\title{Nullspace Networks and Adversarial Attacks in Limited-Angle Computed Tomography}
\author{Noah }
\date{June 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Neural networks that post-process a classical reconstruction are a promising approach to
limited-angle computed tomography, but they are known to be vulnerable to small, deliberate
perturbations of the measurements. This thesis asks whether the Nullspace Network, which may only
add structure that the measurements cannot see and is therefore exactly data consistent, is more
robust than an conventional, unconstrained residual U-Net. Both networks correct the same truncated
pseudoinverse reconstruction and are attacked by projected gradient ascent with an $\ell_2$ budget
equal to the measurement noise and confined to the subspace in which the noise lives. Decomposing
every error into its null-space and range components shows that a plain attack on the Nullspace
Network only measures the noise amplification of the pseudoinverse, which the network inherits and
cannot influence. We therefore introduce  an attack, that is restricted to the null-space, where both networks can act upon.
At a relative noise level of $1\%$, the null-space attack, raises the median null-space error of the Nullspace Network from $0.69\%$ to $38\%$
of the norm of the ground truth.
%TODO: for the residual U-Net, the error increases from... to 43%.
against $43\%$ for the residual U-Net. In $18$ experiments over four
noise levels, four truncation thresholds and two families of phantoms, the null-space attack raises the
null-space error of the Nullspace Network at least eightfold and to the order of magnitude it reaches
for the residual U-Net. Neither of the two networks performs consistently better than the other.
Data consistency alone thus does not make the learned correction robust.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Computed tomography (CT) is an important diagnostic tool in modern medicine, used to identify a wide range of injuries and diseases inside the body, for example to screen the chest for viral and bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis or lung cancer, and the bones and teeth for fractures and infections \parencite{CT-Scan}. Reconstructing an image of the tissue from the measurements is an inverse problem, which is usually solved with the filtered back projection. When applied to measurements from a limited-angle CT scan, however, this reconstruction produces artifacts and unsatisfactory image quality \parencite{CT-LA-CNN}. Because the limited-angle problem is severely ill-posed, an accurate reconstruction method for this setting has yet to be found. Reconstruction with neural networks is a promising approach, since neural networks have been used successfully for many kinds of inverse problems.
One major disadvantage of such reconstruction methods, however, is their vulnerability to deliberate manipulations of the input \parencite{instab}, which has also been observed for limited-angle tomography specifically \parencite{huang2018robustness}.
%TODO: Check reference for existence and validity.
Since CT scans are performed all over the world, a vulnerable reconstruction method would pose a risk to patient safety. This is why this thesis investigates the robustness of such reconstruction methods.\\
%TODO: Read the evidence below!
The evidence on this point is not one-sided. Genzel et al.\ found standard reconstruction networks to be about as robust against adversarial perturbations as total-variation minimisation \parencite{genzel2023robustness}, and Darestani et al.\ found learned methods no less robust than classical sparsity-based ones \parencite{darestani2021measuring}. Gottschling et al.\ trace instabilities and hallucinations to the kernel of the forward operator whenever it is nontrivial \parencite{gottschling2025troublesome}, and Bhadra et al.\ define hallucinations as the part of a reconstruction error that lies in that kernel \parencite{bhadra2021hallucinations}. In limited-angle CT the kernel is large. Architectures that treat it explicitly, such as the Nullspace Network studied here, are therefore natural candidates for a more robust reconstruction, and whether they are is the question of this thesis.\\
Before we describe how the reconstruction method works and how we attack it, we first need to understand how computed tomography, and limited-angle computed tomography in particular, actually works.
\subsection{Limited-angle CT}
To understand how a CT scan works, let us first look at conventional X-ray imaging. Sending an X-ray through body tissue and measuring its intensity on the other side of the body measures how strongly the tissue along the ray attenuates it, that is, the integral of the tissue's attenuation coefficient along the ray. By arranging X-ray emitters and detectors in a plane and placing the tissue to be investigated between them, an X-ray image can be produced.\\
CT relies on the same mechanism of tissue attenuating X-rays according to its density. There are two main ways of doing CT, fan-beam and parallel-beam. Since fan-beam CT is the more complex of the two, we restrict ourselves to parallel-beam CT, and whenever CT is mentioned in this work, parallel-beam CT is meant. In CT, the emitters and detectors lie in the slice plane, perpendicular to the rotation axis. Rotating this array of X-ray sources and detectors around the tissue yields one set of measurements per angle; Figure~\ref{fig:parallel-beam} shows the resulting parallel-beam geometry. Usually the array is rotated through the full $180^\circ$ so that no features are missed. The measurements can be arranged into one 2D image called a sinogram, in which each row corresponds to one angle and each column to one detector. From the sinogram, the axial image, or slice, is then reconstructed.

\\
The number of measurements taken during a CT scan depends on the number of angles and the number of detectors. More measurements mean a longer scan and a higher radiation dose for the patient.\\
Research on ways to reduce the number of measurements while still producing sufficiently accurate reconstructions is therefore of both economic and medical interest. One way to reduce the number of measurements is to measure at fewer angles while retaining the total angular coverage, for example by measuring in steps of $2^\circ$ instead of $1^\circ$, which halves the number of measurements.\\
%TODO: Check references and theis claim!!!
Another way, and the focus of this work, is to reduce the total range of angles covered. Instead of measuring across the full range from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$, one measures only from $0^\circ$ to $120^\circ$. This is called limited-angle CT\@. Limited angular ranges are also imposed by the hardware in clinical practice; digital breast tomosynthesis, for example, covers only between $15^\circ$ and $50^\circ$ \parencite{winter2021dbt}. The problem is severely ill-posed \parencite{davison1983illconditioned}: only those edges of the object whose normals point along a measured direction are stably recovered \parencite{quinto1993singularities}, and methods like the filtered back projection, which work well for conventional CT scans, produce characteristic streak artifacts and fail to provide good reconstructions \parencite{CT-LA-CNN,frikel2013artifacts}.\\
The reconstruction method used in this thesis combines a neural network with a classical reconstruction step, one of the standard ways of bringing learning into inverse problems \parencite{arridge2019solving}. Classical reconstruction methods are better understood and provide a good initial reconstruction, which serves as a useful input to the neural network. The network then only needs to learn the difference between the ground truth and the classical reconstruction.
\subsection{Pseudoinverse}\label{sec:pseudoinverse}
The Radon transform models the X-ray measurement process. It maps a function to its integrals along lines and is defined as
\[\mathcal{R}f(L):=\int_L f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x},\]
where $f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ is an integrable function on the image domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ and $L$ is the line along which the measurement is taken.
Since in computed tomography a line is determined by an offset $s$ and an angle $\theta$, we can also write
\[\mathcal{R}f(\theta,s):=\int_\mathbb{R}f(s\omega_\theta + t\omega_\theta^\perp)\,dt, \qquad \omega_\theta=(\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta}).\]
The Radon transform is linear, since the integral is. Suppose now that $f$ is represented in a finite pixel basis and that we take only $m\in\mathbb{N}$ measurements $(\theta_i, s_i)$, $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$. Then the measurement process can be modelled by a matrix. Numbering the pixels $\Omega_1,\dots,\Omega_n$ of the image, we write
\[f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^n c_j\varphi_j(\mathbf{x}),\qquad \varphi_j:=\mathds{1}_{\Omega_j},\quad \mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\]
as a weighted sum of the indicator functions of the pixels, and collect the weights in a vector $c\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Applying the Radon transform and using its linearity gives for the measurement $y_i$ belonging to $(\theta_i,s_i)$
\[y_i = \mathcal{R}f(\theta_i,s_i) = \sum_j c_j\mathcal{R}\varphi_j(\theta_i,s_i)=\sum_j\mathbf{A}_{ij}c_j=e_i^T\mathbf{A}c,\]
with $\mathbf{A}_{ij}:=\mathcal{R}\varphi_j(\theta_i,s_i)$. Stacking all $m$ measurements into one vector gives the linear system
\[y=\mathbf{A}c.\]
The parameters $\theta_i$ and $s_i$ of the measurements, together with the choice of basis functions, uniquely define $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$.\\
Having the matrix explicitly simplifies the implementation and allows us to reconstruct the image with the pseudoinverse $\mathbf{A}^{+}$. Because of its memory and computational requirements, this approach is not used in clinical CT\@. Clinical images have $512 \times 512$ pixels, and low-dose clinical CT works with $700$ to $900$ detector elements and about $1000$ projections per $360^\circ$ rotation, which corresponds to $500$ projections per $180^\circ$ \parencite{Flohr2013}. This would give a matrix of size $350\,000\times262\,144$, whose singular value decomposition takes far too long to be feasible in clinical practice. In our laboratory setting the matrix is smaller, and having it at hand makes concepts like the null space and its orthogonal complement explicit. This will be important when we discuss Nullspace Networks and the decomposition of the error into the null space and its complement.\\
The pseudoinverse is constructed from the singular value decomposition, which generalises the eigenvalue decomposition to non-square matrices. A general matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ can be written as
\[\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^*,\]
where $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is a diagonal matrix carrying $r$ positive real numbers $\sigma_i$ on its diagonal, where $r$ is the rank of $\mathbf{A}$.
We fix the ordering once and use it for the rest of this work. The singular values are sorted in descending order,
\[\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_r>0,\]
and the columns of $\mathbf{U}$ and of $\mathbf{V}$ are ordered to match, so
that the $i$-th column of each belongs to $\sigma_i$. Two things depend on this
convention. $\sigma_1=\sigma_{\max}=\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2$ is the operator
norm, against which the truncation threshold introduced below is measured; and ``the first $k$ columns of $\mathbf{V}$'' is then an unambiguous
description of the vectors a truncation keeps. The decomposition returned by
$\texttt{torch.linalg.svd}$ is already sorted this way. The pseudoinverse of $\mathbf{A}$ is then
\[\mathbf{A}^+=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{+}\mathbf{U}^*=\mathbf{V}
\begin{pNiceArray}{ccc|c}[margin, cell-space-limits=2pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sigma_1} &        &                     & \Block{3-1}{\text{\Huge 0}} \\
                    & \Ddots &                     &                             \\
                    &        & \dfrac{1}{\sigma_r} &                             \\
\midrule
\Block{1-3}{\text{\Huge 0}} & & & \text{\Huge 0}                                  \\
\end{pNiceArray}\mathbf{U}^*\]
The pseudoinverse maps a sinogram back to an axial image. Real-world measurements are never free of noise, so to represent them faithfully we add noise to the simulated data, and noisy data reveal a problem of the pseudoinverse. Because the problem is severely ill-posed,
%TODO: Check citation and claim
the singular values $\sigma_i$ decay rapidly towards zero \parencite{louis1986incomplete}, so the singular values $1/\sigma_i$ of the pseudoinverse grow just as rapidly. White noise has, in expectation, the same energy in every singular direction, so the pseudoinverse amplifies it most in exactly those directions where the signal is weakest, and the amplified noise can overshadow the signal. To counteract this one uses a truncated pseudoinverse, which sets all singular values below a threshold, $\sigma_i<\tau\sigma_{\max}$, to zero. These remain zero when inverting, so the amplification is bounded by $1/(\tau\sigma_{\max})$.\\
This gives rise to a nontrivial null space $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ i.e.\ nontrivial structures that, when measured, give $0$ measurements. This allows us to decompose the space $\mathbb{R}^n$ into $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ and ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp$ which will be of use later in this work.\\

\subsection{Residual U-Net}\label{sec:resunet}
Before we discuss the key property of a residual network, we need to understand how a general neural network is built.
\subsubsection{Neural Networks}
A neural network consists of connected neurons, which are usually grouped into layers.
Each neuron has several weighted inputs and a bias of its own. The weighted inputs and the bias are summed and passed to an activation function, whose output is the output of the neuron.
The activation function is usually nonlinear. One neuron can therefore be modelled as
\[y = \phi\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i + b\Big),\qquad w_i,x_i,b\in\mathbb{R},\]
where $y$ is the output, $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the activation function, $w_i x_i$ are the weighted inputs and $b$ is the bias of that neuron.
The layers of a neural network are evaluated in sequence: the input layer receives the network input and feeds its output into the next layer. How the layers are connected is an architectural choice.
A fully connected layer feeds the output of every neuron of the preceding layer into every neuron of the layer itself. Each weight belongs to one input of one neuron. A layer of $m$ neurons reading $n$ inputs therefore holds the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and the bias vector $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$. No two of its $nm+m$ parameters are tied to one another \parencite{Goodfellow}.
A different approach, the so-called convolutional layer, acts like a convolution with a small kernel.
To understand it, it is best to think of the neurons of a layer as a grid, so that we can speak of a neuron of one layer and the neurons of the next layer that neighbour it.
A convolutional layer then differs from a fully connected one in two respects \parencite{Goodfellow}. It is sparsely connected, in that a neuron receives input only from a small neighbourhood of the corresponding position in the previous layer. In addition, its weights are shared, in that the same small set of weights, the kernel, is reused at every position of the grid.
The second property is what makes the layer a convolution rather than merely a sparse fully connected layer. It also makes the number of parameters independent of the resolution and ties it to the kernel size alone, and it makes the layer equivariant under translations of its input, which is the natural property to ask of a layer that processes an image \parencite{LeCun1998}.
Layer by layer, the hidden layers are computed according to these connections until the data reach the output layer, which produces the network output.\\
\subsubsection{Residual networks}
Up to some point, deeper models perform better than shallow ones \parencite{deeper-better}. Nevertheless,
it has been reported that adding more layers to a suitably deep model increases its training error \parencite{res}.
This is surprising, as it is easy to write down a deeper model that performs on par with the shallow one:
it suffices to append layers that compute the identity to the shallow model.
The resulting model is deeper but performs exactly as well as the shallow one it was built from.
This indicates that some deep networks are harder to optimise than shallow ones, and that the degradation is a
failure of optimisation rather than a lack of expressive power \parencite{res-org}.
Residual networks address this degradation problem by learning the residual instead of the whole mapping. They were
introduced for image classification, where every layer is convolutional, and the residual connection is what made
networks of a hundred and more convolutional layers trainable at all \parencite{res-org}. The two ideas, convolution
and residual connection, are independent of one another, and it is the residual connection alone that we borrow here;
the convolutional part of our architecture is contributed by the U-Net of Section~\ref{sec:unet}.
Let $\mathcal{H}:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}$ be the true mapping from measurements to images, and let $\mathcal{I}:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}$ be the identity
if measurements and images live in the same space, and otherwise an easily computed classical reconstruction map.
The residual mapping learned by the neural network is then given by $\mathcal{G}:= \mathcal{H} - \mathcal{I}$ \parencite{res-org}.\\
In our setting the two spaces differ, so for $\mathcal{I}$, the classical reconstruction
map, we choose the truncated pseudoinverse $\mathbf{A}^+$. The residual is learned
by a network $N_\theta:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}$ acting on the initial reconstruction. Writing
$\mathbf{x}_\text{init}:=\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{y}$, the full reconstruction map is
\[\mathcal{F}_\theta:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X},\qquad
  \mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}_\text{init} + N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init}).\]
Note that the residual mapping we use differs from the definition above.
Instead of defining the network from $\mathbf{Y}$ to $\mathbf{X}$ we define it from $\mathbf{X}$ to $\mathbf{X}$ and
compose it with the truncated pseudoinverse $\mathbf{A}^{+}$. There are three reasons for this.
The first is architectural. $N_\theta$ is a U-Net, and a U-Net maps a grid to a grid of the same shape; a map from
$\mathbf{Y}$ to $\mathbf{X}$ would have to change shape from $(n_\theta,n_d)$ to $(H,W)$, which convolutions do not
do and which would have to be paid for with fully connected layers.
The second is that $\mathbf{A}^{+}$ is already known. The inversion is an ill-posed but explicitly solved linear
problem, so nothing is gained by having the network learn it again from data; letting the classical step perform the
inversion leaves the network with the genuinely hard part, the artifacts the limited angular range produces.
The third is that this is what makes the two architectures of this work comparable at all. Both the
$\mathcal{F}_\theta$ of this section and the Nullspace Network of Section~\ref{sec:nsn} are the same
$\mathbf{x}_\text{init}$ plus a correction computed from it, and they differ only in whether that correction is
constrained. Had the network consumed the sinogram directly, there would be no $\mathbf{x}_\text{init}$ for a
null-space correction to be added to.
We refer to $N_\theta$ as the network and to $\mathcal{F}_\theta$ as the reconstruction
method. Both symbols are used with these meanings for the remainder of this work.\\
\subsubsection{U-Net}\label{sec:unet}
So far we have only discussed the overall architecture of the reconstruction method.
We now turn to the architecture of the neural network itself.
The network we use is a U-Net, a convolutional neural network that maps images to images.
The U-Net consists of two paths, a contracting and an expansive one.
The contracting path is built on the repeated application of convolutions,
each followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU), and of max pooling operations for downsampling \parencite{U-NET}.\\
As mentioned before, a convolutional layer restricts which neurons of the next layer receive the output of a neuron of the current layer.
With a kernel of size $3\times3$, a neuron can only influence the neuron at its own position and its $8$ neighbours.
The ReLU is a popular activation function. It is the identity for positive inputs and maps everything else to $0$.
Max pooling slides a small window across the grid and replaces the values under the window by their maximum. It
therefore keeps, at each position, only the strongest response in a neighbourhood and discards where within that
neighbourhood it occurred. How far the window advances between two positions is the stride, and the stride alone
decides the size of the output. A $4\times4$ input under a $2\times2$ window gives a $2\times2$ output at stride $2$,
where the windows tile the input without overlap, and a $3\times3$ output at stride $1$, where consecutive windows
share a column.
\[
\begin{pNiceMatrix}[margin]
\Block[draw=red,fill=red!15]{2-2}{}
\sigma_1    & \sigma_2    & \sigma_3 \Block[draw=red,fill=red!30]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_4\\
\sigma_5    & \sigma_6    & \sigma_7    & \sigma_8\\
\sigma_9 \Block[draw=red]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_{10} & \sigma_{11}\Block[draw=red]{2-2}{}
 & \sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{13} & \sigma_{14} & \sigma_{15} & \sigma_{16}
\end{pNiceMatrix}
\begin{pNiceMatrix}[margin]
\Block[draw=blue,fill=blue!15]{2-2}{}
\sigma_1    & \sigma_2   \Block[draw=blue,fill=blue!30]{2-2}{} & \sigma_3 \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_4\\
\sigma_5  \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}  & \sigma_6  \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}  & \sigma_7 \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}   & \sigma_8\\
\sigma_9 \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_{10}\Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{} & \sigma_{11}\Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}
 & \sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{13} & \sigma_{14} & \sigma_{15} & \sigma_{16}
\end{pNiceMatrix}
\]
The illustration shows both cases. The red windows produce a $2\times 2$ grid of values,
since the window moves by two entries (stride $2$) between steps and therefore never covers an entry twice.
The blue windows produce a $3\times 3$ grid, since the window moves by one entry (stride $1$) and therefore covers some entries several times.
The U-Net uses stride $2$, which reduces the number of neurons by a factor of $4$. The number of
weights is untouched by this, since it depends on the kernel size and the channel counts alone.
The memory gained by carrying fewer values is spent on new feature channels.
A convolutional layer does not hold one grid of numbers but a stack of them, and the members of that stack are the
feature channels. Each channel is produced by its own filter, that is, by one kernel per input channel, convolved with
the whole input stack and summed. A layer taking $c_\text{in}$ channels to $c_\text{out}$ channels therefore holds
$c_\text{out}$ filters of $c_\text{in}$ kernels each, and the $i$-th output channel records how strongly the $i$-th
filter responds at each position of the grid \parencite{Goodfellow}. The channel count is thus the number of
distinct local patterns a layer can report on at once, and it is the dial by which the capacity of a convolutional
layer is set, the resolution being fixed by the data. Early layers typically respond to edges and simple oriented
structure, later ones to their combinations \parencite{LeCun1998}.
The $2\times2$ max pooling halves the spatial resolution but leaves the number of channels unchanged.
The number of feature channels is then doubled by the first convolution of the following block, which uses twice as many filters as that block has input channels.
After the contractive path, the expansive path applies a form of upsampling with another $2\times 2$ convolution, halving the number of feature channels.
The upsampled data are then concatenated with the data of the corresponding step of the contracting path and convolved twice more, each convolution followed by a ReLU\@.
The original U-Net has $23$ convolutional layers \parencite{U-NET}. It was designed for image segmentation,
but it is equally applicable to other image-to-image tasks.
\subsection{Nullspace Network}\label{sec:nsn}
The residual U-Net is an unconstrained network: it can modify its input in whatever way it likes to produce its output.
This can lead to problems, since it can produce predictions that do not agree with the measurements it was given.
One remedy is the Nullspace Network of Schwab et al.\ \parencite{NSL}. Its architecture is identical to that of the residual U-Net, except
that the output of the network is projected onto the null space of the forward operator, that is, of the measurement process, before it is added.
This guarantees data consistency, and one may hope that the resulting network is also more robust to adversarial attacks.
As the results of this work show, robustness to adversarial attacks
does not come for free with data consistency: None of the two networks is consistently more robust than the other.\\
%TODO: Check citations and claims
Related approaches restrict learning to the part of limited-angle data that the measurement cannot determine, for example to
the invisible shearlet coefficients \parencite{bubba2019invisible}, or relax the constraint so that the network may also
correct the measured part within a controlled distance of the data \parencite{goeppel2023dataproximal}; the latter has been
compared with a residual and a Nullspace Network for limited-view CT \parencite{goeppel2025bvm}.\\
To use a Nullspace Network, one needs a projection onto the null space of the forward operator.
This is where the singular value decomposition is useful once more,
since the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{V}$ gives the null-space projection directly.
With the descending ordering of Section~\ref{sec:pseudoinverse}, the $k$
directions that are kept are the \emph{first} $k$ columns of $\mathbf{V}$, and
collecting them in the matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ allows us to calculate the projection $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ by
\[\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^*\]
The correction of the Nullspace Network is then the composition
$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\circ N_\theta$. It is applied to an initial reconstruction $\mathbf{x}_\text{init}$ obtained by some classical
reconstruction method, in our case the truncated pseudoinverse $\mathbf{A}^{+}$:
\[\mathbf{x}_\text{out} = \mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}_\text{init} + \mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init}).\]
Applying $\mathbf{A}$ to this equation shows that the network output cannot change the measurements of the initial reconstruction,
\[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})} = 0 \implies \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\text{out} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\text{init}.\]
This identity is exact for the untruncated operator. Under the truncation of
Section~\ref{sec:pinv} the projector retains the discarded small-singular-value directions and what
vanishes exactly is the measured trace $\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$;
Section~\ref{sec:nsn-impl} states the difference and why the measured version is the one the
reconstruction relies on.
The network is provably data consistent. This means measuring an image produced by the Nullspace Network will always yield the exact measurements used for that prediction.
The flip side is that any error in its input that produces nonzero measurements can never be corrected by the network.\\
Some noise is always present in the measurements. It survives the pseudoinverse and produces nonzero measurements, so the error it causes is beyond the reach of the Nullspace Network.
Decomposing the reconstruction error $\mathbf{e}:=\mathbf{x}_\text{out}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger$, where $\mathbf{x}^\dagger$ is the ground truth, into its null-space component and its component in the orthogonal complement makes this precise.
The complement ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp=\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}^{*})$ is the part of image space that the measurements determine, and we therefore call $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})\mathbf{e}$ the \emph{range component} of the error.
For the Nullspace Network the range component is identical to that of the initial reconstruction, whatever the network predicts.
We refer to it as the \emph{range floor}. In the range component the Nullspace Network can therefore never match the unconstrained residual network,
which is free to correct this component and does.\\
The projection decomposes the space of reconstructions into the null space $\mathcal{N}$ and its complement $\mathcal{N}^\perp$.
We call the two subspaces the null-space \emph{channel} and the range \emph{channel} of the error.
The decomposition is used both to analyse our attacks and to construct the new type of attack introduced in this work.

Its null-space component is what Bhadra et al.\ call a null-space hallucination \parencite{bhadra2021hallucinations}:
structure that agrees with the measurement but is not present in the object.
\subsection{Adversarial Attacks}
An adversarial attack is the deliberate construction of a small perturbation of a specific input to a model that maximises the error of its output, here the error of the reconstruction.

Such perturbations were first described for image classifiers \parencite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2015explaining}.
Attacks differ significantly depending on how this error is measured and how much the threat model lets the attacker know about the model \parencite{advers}.
In this work we use adversarial attacks to investigate the robustness of the residual U-Net and the Nullspace Network.
The next section describes adversarial attacks in more detail and explains the modifications we made to the general attack framework to evaluate our models more fairly.\\
\section{Theory behind Adversarial Attacks}
Let $\mathcal{F}_\theta:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}$ denote any reconstruction method going from the space of measurements to
the space of images. This section describes the methods we use to attack $\mathcal{F}_\theta$.

\subsection{Threat model and projected gradient ascent}\label{sec:attacks}
An adversarial attack depends heavily on the threat model, which defines how much information the attacker is given and how the success of the attack is measured.
The attacks investigated in this work are so-called white-box attacks. Unlike a black-box attacker, a white-box attacker has complete knowledge of the reconstruction method;
in our case the attacker knows the exact model architecture as well as its weights. Such an attack is much more powerful than a black-box attack,
in which the attacker has no knowledge of the reconstruction method. As mentioned before, the aim is to find a small perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}^* \in\mathbf{Y}$ defined by
\[\boldsymbol{\delta}^*\;=\argmax_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_p \leq \varepsilon}\;\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta}), \mathbf{x}^\dagger ),\]
where $\mathbf{y}$ are the measurements, $\mathbf{x}^\dagger$ is the ground truth corresponding to them,
$\mathcal{L}$ is a loss function and $\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is the budget of the attack.
Adversarial attacks use different strategies to find such a perturbation. In this work we rely on projected gradient descent (PGD), which
Madry et al.\ \parencite{PGD} argue is the strongest attack that uses only local first-order information about the network.\\\\
Projected gradient descent is a general method for minimising a differentiable function over
a closed convex set: one takes a gradient step and projects the result back onto the set.
Suppose we want to attack the pair $(\mathbf{x}^\dagger, \mathbf{y})$ of ground truth and sinogram. For this attack the closed convex set is the ball of radius $\varepsilon$ around the clean sinogram,
\[\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon:=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{Y}\;:\;\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2\leq\varepsilon\},\]
which ensures that every iterate stays within the budget of the attack.
A PGD attack starts from a random point $\mathbf{y}_0\in\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon$,
and each step is computed as
\[\mathbf{y}_{n+1}:=\Pi_{\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon}\left( \mathbf{y}_n-\alpha\mathbf{g}_n\right), \qquad \mathbf{g}_n:=\nabla_\mathbf{z}\, \mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{z}),\,\mathbf{x}^\dagger\right)   \Big|_{\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}_n}\]
where $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ is the step size, $\Pi_{\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon}$ is the
projection onto that ball and $\mathcal{L}$ is the same loss function as above. Note that
the ball is centred at $\mathbf{y}$ and not at the origin: we are looking for a small
perturbation of one specific sinogram, not for a sinogram of small norm.\\
Two changes turn this minimiser into the attack we use. First, we maximise instead of
minimise, so the gradient step is added rather than subtracted. Second, the raw gradient is
replaced by the direction of steepest ascent with respect to the norm in which the budget is
measured. For a step of length $\alpha$ that direction maximises
$\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{d}\rangle$ over $\lVert\mathbf{d}\rVert\leq\alpha$, and it therefore
depends on which norm is written there:
\[\ell_\infty:\quad \alpha\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{g}),\qquad\qquad
  \ell_2:\quad \alpha\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert_2}.\]
Every attack in this work is measured in the $\ell_2$ norm, so it is the second of the two that
we use. The first is given to explain the form in which the algorithm is usually stated.
Madry et al.~\parencite{PGD} write it with the sign function because their threat model is
bounded in the $\ell_\infty$ norm, which suits images whose pixels are perturbed independently.
A sinogram is not of that kind. The perturbation we admit is itself a measurement, so what
bounds it is the energy an attacker may add to the scan, and that is an $\ell_2$ quantity; it is
also the norm in which the simulated measurement noise of Section~\ref{sec:data} is prescribed,
which is what makes attack and noise comparable. Step rule and constraint set are
therefore both expressed in the $\ell_2$ norm.\\
A last adaptation concerns the feasible set. We investigate only those perturbations of the
measurements that an attacker could realistically produce, that is, perturbations which are
themselves measurements taken at the angles the scanner actually covers. We therefore
additionally require the perturbation to lie in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$, which
gives the feasible set $\mathcal{C}:=\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})\cap B_\varepsilon$, where
$B_\varepsilon$ is now the ball of radius $\varepsilon$ about the origin. It is convenient to write
the iteration in terms of the perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}_n:=\mathbf{y}_n-\mathbf{y}$ rather
than of the sinogram itself. 
Doing so changes nothing about the algorithm. The substitution is
the translation by the fixed vector $-\mathbf{y}$, and a translation moves iterate and constraint
set together: $\mathbf{y}_n$ lies in $\mathbf{y}+\mathcal{C}$ exactly when
$\boldsymbol{\delta}_n$ lies in $\mathcal{C}$, so
$\Pi_{\mathbf{y}+\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{y}+\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{v})$.
The gradient is likewise unchanged, since $\mathcal{L}$ depends on $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ only
through $\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta}$ and the chain rule contributes the identity. The two
iterations therefore produce the same sinograms, and the update rule becomes
\[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n+1}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\left( \boldsymbol{\delta}_n+\alpha\frac{\mathbf{g}_n}{\lVert\mathbf{g}_n\rVert_2}\right),\qquad \mathbf{g}_n:=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta}),\,\mathbf{x}^\dagger\right)\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_n},\]
with the starting point $\boldsymbol{\delta}_0$ drawn at random from $\mathcal{C}$.
This is the blueprint for every attack we use to test the robustness of our models;
the attacks differ only in the choice of the loss $\mathcal{L}$.\\
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks on Subspaces}\label{sec:subspace}
With the blueprint we have just given, it is already possible to perform successful attacks
on both models. During our work, however, we noticed that when attacking a Nullspace
Network with the plain squared-error loss, the error found by the attack grew almost
entirely in the range component. By the range floor of Section~\ref{sec:nsn} this is
precisely the component the network does not control: it is inherited unchanged from the
initial reconstruction, so an attack that grows it is exploiting the architecture rather
than the learned reconstruction. The resulting error is large, but it is not evidence of a
vulnerability, and it is not comparable to what the same attack achieves against the
unconstrained residual network, which is free to correct that component.\\
We addressed this issue by changing the loss function $\mathcal{L}$ we use in the attack. Let
\[\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{\delta}):=\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\]
denote the reconstruction error. Instead of its total energy we maximise the energy of a
single component of its decomposition into the two subspaces $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ and ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp$,
\[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}:=\lVert \mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\rVert_2^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp}:=\lVert (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})\,\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\rVert_2^2 .\]
Both projections are linear and differentiable, so the attack of the previous section
applies unchanged; only the loss whose gradient is taken is replaced. Maximising
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ forces the attack to damage the null-space component, which is
the only part of the reconstruction the Nullspace Network can actually modify. The residual network is free to act in this
component as well, so restricting the attack to it compares the two networks on the ground where both of them work.
Maximising $\mathcal{L}_{{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp}$ gives the complementary attack and serves as a control. Against the Nullspace Network
it can only ever attack the pseudoinverse. Against the residual U-Net it shows how far a small perturbation can move the measured part of the reconstruction.
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks using Examples}\label{sec:targeted}
A second modification alters the attack objective so that it no longer drives the reconstruction \emph{away} from the ground truth,
but \emph{towards} a prescribed image. Attacks of this kind are called targeted attacks.
Let $\mathbf{t}\in\mathbf{X}$ be a target image and replace the loss by
\[\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{t}:=-\lVert\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathbf{t}\rVert_2^2 .\]
Because of the minus sign in front of the norm, maximising $\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{t}$ minimises the distance between the reconstruction and the target.
The update rule of the previous section can again be applied unchanged.\\
We use two different targets in our attacks. The first is the zero image, $\mathbf{t}=0$, which asks how
far towards an empty reconstruction an attacker can push the model within the budget. The
second is the ground truth of a different sample, so that the attack attempts to
make the reconstruction of one object resemble another. It asks not whether the reconstruction can be corrupted, but
whether it can be corrupted into something that still looks like a plausible scan of a
different patient. A targeted attack is in general a harder problem than an untargeted one,
because the attacker must reach one particular point of image space rather than any point
far from the ground truth, and we should expect correspondingly smaller errors at the same
budget.
\section{Numerical implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
This section describes how the operators, networks and attacks of the previous
two sections are realised in code. The implementation is written in Python and
rests on two libraries: the ASTRA Toolbox \parencite{Astra} provides the tomographic geometry and
PyTorch \parencite{Pytorch} provides the tensor algebra,
the neural network layers and reverse-mode automatic differentiation.
The central object of the implementation is the tensor: a
multidimensional array that carries a shape, a numeric type and the device it
lives on (CPU or GPU).
In addition, a tensor can record the operations that produced it. That record is the
computational graph, and it is what lets PyTorch differentiate a value with respect to
any tensor it was computed from. The rule by which it does so is reverse-mode automatic
differentiation.
A program that computes a scalar $\mathcal{L}$ from an input $\mathbf{z}$ is a composition
$\mathcal{L}=f_N\circ\dots\circ f_1(\mathbf{z})$ of the elementary operations it executed,
so by the chain rule its gradient is the product of the transposed Jacobians
\[\nabla_{\!\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}
 =\mathbf{J}_{f_1}^{*}\cdots\mathbf{J}_{f_{N-1}}^{*}\,\nabla f_N,\]
where the last factor is a vector because $f_N$ is scalar-valued.
Reverse mode evaluates this product from right to left, that is, from the output
backwards, so that every factor is applied to a vector rather than to a matrix and no
Jacobian is ever assembled. The cost is then a small multiple of the cost of the forward
computation, independently of how many inputs there are. 
%PersonalTODO: Look more into forward and backward mode
Forward mode, which multiplies
from the other end, would instead cost one pass per input. Since our loss is one number
and our input is a sinogram of $21\,840$ measured entries, reverse mode is what makes a gradient
affordable at all. Each operation contributes the transpose of its own Jacobian, which
for the matrix products of Section~\ref{sec:pinv} is again a matrix product, and this is
why the gradient of an attack can travel from the reconstruction back into the
measurements.
Every number reported in this thesis was produced with PyTorch 2.3.1 on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000.\\

Two shapes occur throughout. An image is a tensor of shape $(B,C,H,W)$ (batch,
channel, height and width of the image), and a sinogram is a tensor of shape
$(B,C,n_\theta,n_d)$ (batch, channel, projection angle, detector element).
Here $B$ is the number of samples processed simultaneously and $C=1$, since our
images are greyscale. Every operator described below maps between these two
shapes and acts on a whole batch in a single call.\\
One convention deserves to be stated in advance, because it recurs in every
later section. The limited-angle operators do not return a shortened sinogram
containing only the measured rows; they return a full-shape sinogram in
which the rows belonging to unmeasured angles are set to zero. Every sinogram in
a run therefore has the same shape, whether it was produced by the full or by
the limited-angle operator, and the restriction to the measured angles is itself
an operator, namely the multiplication with a $0/1$ mask, which we denote by
$\mathbf{M}$. The mask $\mathbf{M}$ is a tensor of shape
$(n_\theta,n_d)$ with $1$ in the rows corresponding to measured angles and $0$
elsewhere.\\
It is important to note that $\mathbf{M}$ is not the same thing as
$\mathbf{P}_k$, the projector onto the retained range $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$. Zeroing the unmeasured
rows says only that a sinogram is supported on the angles the scanner covers, not that it lies in the retained range.
The sinograms that are measurements of an image form $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$,
a proper subspace of $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M})$, and those of them that
survive the truncation form $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$, a proper
subspace again. This gives the chain of subspaces
\[\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)\subset \operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})\subset\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M}).\]
For the geometry of this work the three have dimensions
$11\,150$, $16\,384$ and $21\,840$. The middle one, $16\,384$, is the column rank of
$\mathbf{A}$, which is full, so it equals the number of pixels rather than the
number of measured detector readings. Only the outermost and the innermost occur
in the implementation. $\mathbf{M}$ defines the shape convention just described,
and $\mathbf{P}_k=\mathbf{U}_k\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$ is the projection onto
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$. It dictates the
simulated noise of Section~\ref{sec:data}, the adversarial perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:adversarial} and the data-consistency residual of
Section~\ref{sec:metrics} alike.\\
The two retained factors are used in different spaces and never
interchangeably. $\mathbf{U}_k$ is $m\times k$ and spans the retained part of
sinogram space, so everything acting on measurements is built from it.
$\mathbf{P}_k$ and the left half of the pseudoinverse. $\mathbf{V}_k$ is
$n\times k$ and spans $\operatorname{row}(\mathbf{A})$, the orthogonal
complement of the null space in image space, so everything acting on images is
built from it: $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$,
the error decomposition, and the right half of the pseudoinverse.\\
\subsection{Radon operator}\label{sec:radon}
The Radon operator is implemented with the ASTRA Toolbox \parencite{Astra},
which is developed at the University of Antwerp and CWI Amsterdam and supports a range of tomographic geometries.
For this work we use a 2D parallel-beam geometry.
The first step towards a Radon operator in the ASTRA Toolbox is to define a volume geometry.
It fixes the size of the image to be reconstructed, which in turn dictates how many detector elements are needed.
The second descriptor is the projection geometry, which fixes the beam type
(\texttt{parallel}), the spacing of the detector elements, their number, and the
vector of projection angles. From the two descriptors a projector is built using
the \texttt{strip} model, which integrates over the strip that a detector
element actually sees rather than along an infinitely thin line. This is the
discretisation assumed in Section~\ref{sec:pseudoinverse}, a pixel basis on the image
side and a finite set of measurements $(\theta_i,s_i)$ on the sinogram side, with one refinement. The entry $\mathbf{A}_{ij}$ is not the line integral $\mathcal{R}\varphi_j(\theta_i,s_i)$ at the centre of the
detector element but its integral over the width of the element, which is the area in which the strip and pixel $j$ overlap.\\
The geometry used for every experiment in this work is the following.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
image resolution        & $128\times128$ pixels, so $n=16\,384$ \\
detector elements       & $n_d=182$ \\
projection angles       & $n_\theta=180$, equispaced over $\lbrack 0^\circ,180^\circ\rparen$ \\
limited-angle window    & $\lbrack 0^\circ,120^\circ\rparen$, i.e.\ $120$ of the $180$ angles \\
pixel spacing           & $\mathrm{d}x=1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The detector count is chosen as $\lfloor\sqrt2\cdot128\rfloor+1=182$, that is, at
least the length of the image diagonal, so that no corner of the image can fall
outside the detector array for any projection angle.\\
ASTRA is used to construct the system matrix itself rather than as a
matrix-free forward and backprojection pair, because the matrix is what gives
access to exact inversion: with its singular value decomposition at hand the
pseudoinverse and the null-space projection are closed-form linear operators
instead of the output of an iterative solver. Both are also ordinary tensor
products, which is what lets an attack gradient travel through them. In Python
this is implemented in the following way:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
angles = np.linspace(0, 180, 180, endpoint=False) * np.pi / 180  # radians
vol_geom = astra.create_vol_geom(128, 128)
proj_geom = astra.create_proj_geom('parallel', 1.0, 182, angles)
projector_id = astra.create_projector('strip', proj_geom, vol_geom)
matrix_id = astra.projector.matrix(projector_id)
csr = astra.matrix.get(matrix_id)
\end{lstlisting}
The first four lines create a projector, referenced by its id, that computes the Radon transform of an image;
the final two lines turn it into an explicit matrix that performs the same operation.
ASTRA returns the matrix in compressed sparse row (CSR) format, as a
\texttt{scipy.sparse.csr\_matrix}, and it is from this matrix that the pseudoinverse and the
null-space projections are later computed.
The format stores only the nonzero entries, in three arrays: their values, the column index
of each of them, and, for each row, where in the first two arrays that row begins. A row of
$\mathbf{A}$ is one ray and its nonzeros are the pixels that ray crosses, so this is the
natural layout here and it is also the one ASTRA returns. It is worth naming the format
because the choice reappears in Section~\ref{sec:pinv}: reading an entry of a CSR matrix
means reading its position from the index array first, and that indirection is what makes
the sparse layout the slower one at the density we have.
In the following, let $\mathbf{A}_\text{full}:=\texttt{csr}$ be this sparse matrix, of dimensions $182\cdot180\times128\cdot128=32\,760\times16\,384$.
ASTRA orders the rows angle-major, so row $i\cdot n_d+j$ holds detector
$j$ at angle $\theta_i$. Keeping only the rows that belong to the $120$ measured angles gives the limited-angle matrix
$\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{21\,840\times16\,384}$.
It is $\mathbf{A}$ that corresponds to the operator of the theory:
it models the limited-angle measurement process that the networks aim to invert and the attacks aim to perturb.\\
Building the matrices and decomposing them is expensive but depends only on the
geometry, never on the data. Both are therefore cached on disk under a SHA-256
hash of the resolution, detector count, pixel spacing, angle vector,
limited-angle window, truncation threshold and floating-point precision, so that
a later run with the same geometry loads them instead of recomputing them.
Data generation, training and the attacks ask for the same geometry, truncation and
precision, so one cache entry serves all three. The truncation study of
Section~\ref{sec:pinv} is the one exception: it needs every singular direction, decomposes the operator
without truncation and therefore keeps an entry of its own. The singular value decomposition
is thus computed twice per geometry, once in full for the study and once at the truncation
$\tau$ of the run. Neither depends on the noise level, so the four noise levels of
Section~\ref{sec:data} share both entries.\\
\subsection{Pseudoinverse}\label{sec:pinv}
The pseudoinverse of Section~\ref{sec:pseudoinverse} requires the singular value decomposition of
the system matrix, and this is the single most expensive computation in the
whole pipeline. $\mathbf{A}$ is densified and decomposed with
\texttt{torch.linalg.svd} on the GPU\@. The decomposition runs in double precision, for the reason given at the end of this section,
so the dense copy it works on occupies
$21\,840\cdot16\,384\cdot8\,\text{B}=2.9$\,GB\@. Without a GPU the same dense
decomposition runs through LAPACK on the CPU, which is the path the test suite
takes. Everything else, the matrix, the factors in the cache on disk and every
operator applied at training and attack time, is in single precision.\\
The truncation described in Section~\ref{sec:pseudoinverse} is applied relative to the largest
singular value. A direction is retained if
\[\sigma_i\;\geq\;\tau\,\sigma_{\max},\qquad \tau = 4\cdot10^{-3}.\]
Discarding the directions below such a threshold is the truncated singular value
decomposition, the standard regularisation of a discrete ill-posed problem
\parencite{Hansen1987}: a matrix is assigned the numerical rank $r$ for which $\sigma_r\geq\delta>\sigma_{r+1}$,
with the tolerance $\delta$ taken relative to the norm of the matrix \parencite{GolubVanLoan}.
The size of $\tau$ should be consistent with the machine precision when the data are exact.
Our data are not exact.
They carry relative noise of $\sigma_\text{rel}\in\{0.005,\,0.01,\,0.02,\,0.05\}$,
four to five orders of magnitude above the single-precision roundoff
$\epsilon\approx1.2\cdot10^{-7}$ of the paragraph on precision below, so a
cutoff at machine precision would be meaningless here. The value $\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$ sits
just below the smallest of the four noise levels. The heuristic behind this choice is that a
direction whose singular value, relative to $\sigma_{\max}$, lies below the relative noise level is
one whose signal the noise drowns, so the pseudoinverse could only fill it with amplified noise.\\
Another natural choice of $\tau$ is the one for which the pseudoinverse reconstruction has the
smallest error. A truncation study, run as part of every experiment, determines it per noise level
on the first $64$ phantoms of the training set, so that the choice of $\tau$ never sees the test
set. The study needs only one decomposition. The truncations are nested, since a larger $\tau$
keeps a prefix of the singular directions, so with the coefficients $\mathbf{v}_i^{*}\mathbf{x}^\dagger$
of a phantom and $\mathbf{u}_i^{*}\mathbf{g}$ of a noise draw $\mathbf{g}$, the null-space and the
range part of the pseudoinverse error at every $\tau$ follow from cumulative sums over $i$. The
noise is drawn as in Section~\ref{sec:data}, through the range projector of the respective $\tau$.
The $\tau$ found in this way is optimal for the pseudoinverse alone. No network is trained per
$\tau$, so it is not necessarily optimal for the Nullspace Network or the residual network, and the
two need not agree.
%TODO:\parencite Studie von Markus (Big in Japan)
Because the study evaluates a whole range of thresholds, it also shows how strongly the split into
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ and ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp$ depends on where the boundary is drawn;
Section~\ref{sec:results-tau} reports what it finds.\\
Scaling the threshold by $\sigma_{\max}$ rather than fixing
it absolutely puts the cutoff in relation to the operator norm, so $\tau$ is
dimensionless and does not have to be re-tuned when the geometry changes. It also controls the conditioning of
the retained operator directly, and it is worth spelling that out. A retained
direction satisfies $\sigma_i\geq\tau\sigma_{\max}$ by definition, so the
smallest retained singular value obeys $\sigma_{\min}\geq\tau\sigma_{\max}$ and
the condition number of the retained operator is
\[\frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\tau}\;=\;250 .\]
Equivalently $\lVert\mathbf{A}^{+}\rVert_2=1/\sigma_{\min}\leq
1/(\tau\sigma_{\max})$. Measured relative to the operator norm, the
pseudoinverse can amplify by at most $1/\tau$.
So we can use $\tau$ to limit the noise amplification in the pseudoinverse.\\
For the geometry of Section~\ref{sec:radon} this keeps $11\,150$ of $16\,384$ directions. The numerical
null space $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$, which is the space the Nullspace Network operates on, has
dimension $16\,384-11\,150=5\,234$, roughly a third of image space. This is not a
small correction channel but a substantial subspace, and every statement of
Section~\ref{sec:nsn} about what the Nullspace Network can and cannot repair
refers to this splitting of $\mathbb{R}^{16\,384}$.\\
Four implementation details are worth recording.\\
First, the pseudoinverse is never stored by itself. Writing $\mathbf{U}_k,\Sigma_k,
\mathbf{V}_k$ for the retained factors, the reconstruction is evaluated as the
two matrix products \[\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{y} \;=\; \mathbf{V}_k\,\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}\mathbf{y},\]
applied to a batch of measurement vectors. The reason is that the factors have to be stored regardless,
because three further operators are built from them, and once they are in memory
an explicit $\mathbf{A}^{+}$ alongside them would be $1.4$\,GB of pure
duplication. The two factors earn their keep in different spaces, and it is worth separating
them. $\mathbf{V}_k$ (image-domain), which is $n\times k$, is used for the null-space projector
$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$.
$\mathbf{U}_k$ (sinogram-domain), which is $m\times k$, is used for the projector $\mathbf{P}_k=\mathbf{U}_k\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$.\\
Second, the space the Nullspace Network acts in is the numerical null space of the truncated operator, the
$5\,234$-dimensional space counted above, and the space every decomposition
figure of Section~\ref{sec:results} splits along. The error decomposition is also computed without forming the explicit $n\times n$ matrix.
Instead the null-space complement component is calculated using
$\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}=\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}\mathbf{e}$ and the
null-space component is
$\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}=\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$. The two are
orthogonal by construction, so
$\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}\rVert^2+
\lVert\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}\rVert^2$.\\
Third, and decisively for this work, all of these operations are ordinary tensor
products, so PyTorch differentiates them. The gradient of an attack loss with
respect to the sinogram has to travel backwards through the initial
reconstruction into the measurements, and it is only because the pseudoinverse
is two matrix multiplications that this is possible at all.\\
Finally, precision and layout. Everything is single precision and dense. Dense,
because the sparse kernels are considerably slower for a matrix of this density.
The density follows from the geometry rather than from measurement: a row of
$\mathbf{A}$ is one ray, and a ray crossing an image of $w\times w$ pixels
meets on the order of $w$ of its $w^2$ pixels, so the fraction
of nonzero entries is on the order of $1/w$. At $w=128$ that
is about $0.8\,\%$. A sparse kernel has to pay for indirect indexing on every entry it
touches: the value alone does not say where it belongs, so its column has to be read from
a second array first, and only then can the matching entry of the vector be fetched. That
second fetch lands wherever the column index points, so consecutive entries of a row need
not be near one another in memory, and neither the cache nor the coalesced loads a GPU
relies on can be used. A dense kernel does none of this, because position is implied by
the layout. The sparse format wins only when the indirection buys a large enough reduction
in arithmetic to pay for itself; at one per cent of a matrix this size, it does not.
Dense is also preferred because the automatic differentiation support for sparse CSR is still experimental, and the
attack depends on differentiating through these products.\\
Single precision is chosen for the matrices and the stored factors because it halves
the memory of the dense copies without meaningfully deteriorating accuracy. The
decomposition itself is computed in double precision and only its factors are rounded
to single. This ensures the decomposition is orthonormal to  $10^{-7}$, which is the level every number in
Section~\ref{sec:results} was produced at. Every build and every load of the cached factors is
checked against these identities and refused above $10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Data generation}\label{sec:data}
The dataset consists of $5\,000$ phantoms, each containing a single random
ellipse, generated with the \texttt{ellipsoid\_phantom} routine of the Operator
Discretization Library (ODL) \parencite{ODL} on the image grid supplied by the ellipse
dataset of the Deep Inversion Validation Library (DIVAL) \parencite{DIVAL}. ODL rasterises
the ellipse, DIVAL supplies the discretised image space it is
rasterised onto. In the normalised coordinates ${[-1,1]}^2$ in which ODL specifies an ellipse,
the two semi-axes are drawn as $0.2$ times an exponential variable of mean one and redrawn until
the ellipse has an area of at least $0.1$; the intensity is then drawn uniformly from $[0.3,1]$,
the centre uniformly from ${[-0.3,0.3]}^2$ and the rotation uniformly from $\lbrack 0,2\pi\rparen$, and the
whole ellipse is drawn anew until it lies entirely inside the image. Restricting each image to one ellipse is a
deliberate simplification. It keeps the learning problem simple enough that both
architectures reach a small clean error in the component they control, so that what an attack adds
is not masked by what the networks fail to learn.
To test whether the findings carry over to more complex images, the study is repeated at one noise level with the single ellipse replaced by
 the $\operatorname{Poisson}(40)$ overlapping ellipses of DIVAL's standard generator.\\
For every sample the pipeline performs four steps.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draw the ground truth $\mathbf{x}^\dagger$.
    \item Simulate the noise-free measurement $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^\dagger$.
    \item Add noise. A standard normal tensor is drawn on the sinogram and
    projected with $\mathbf{P}_k$, giving $\boldsymbol{\eta}$. $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is then
    rescaled so that the perturbation has a prescribed \emph{relative} size,
    \[\mathbf{y}^\delta=\mathbf{y}+\sigma_\text{rel}
    \frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}\rVert_2}\boldsymbol{\eta},
    \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
    \lVert\mathbf{y}^\delta-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2=\sigma_\text{rel}\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2\]
    holds exactly and per sample, independently of how bright the phantom is.
    \item Compute the initial reconstruction
    $\mathbf{x}_\text{init}=\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{y}^\delta$ and store ground
    truth, sinogram and initial reconstruction to disk.
\end{enumerate}
Consider the chain of subspaces
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)\subset\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})
\subset\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M})$ of the start of Section~\ref{sec:implementation}, and note that the
network never sees $\mathbf{y}^\delta$ itself but only
$\mathbf{x}_\text{init}=\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{y}^\delta$. Since
$\mathbf{A}^{+}=\mathbf{V}_k\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$, any part of the perturbation
orthogonal to $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ is annihilated before it reaches the
reconstruction. Noise drawn in either of the two larger spaces therefore arrives only in
part, and the nominal $\sigma_\text{rel}$ would overstate the perturbation the network is
actually trained against. Energy placed in
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M})\setminus\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$ is a sinogram no
scanner could emit, and energy placed in
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})\setminus\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ is a
measurement the truncation throws away. Drawing it in
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ closes both gaps at once, and the perturbation is then
felt in full.
The calibration this protects is the one the attacks depend on.
Section~\ref{sec:adversarial} grants the attacker a budget of $\sigma_\text{rel}$ and has it spend that
budget entirely inside $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$. Were the noise drawn in a larger
space, the two numbers would no longer describe the same perturbation size.
Step 3 assures we always draw noise from $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$, so the full noise reaches the reconstruction.\\
The difference caused by a draw in the wrong subspace is measured by the truncation study of
Section~\ref{sec:pinv}, which runs with every experiment.
It compares the two schemes on the same phantoms and the same underlying Gaussian draws.
Noise drawn in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$ delivers
$0.825\pm0.004$ of its nominal amplitude to the reconstruction, against $1$ when
it is drawn in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$. This is the value to be expected: an isotropic draw in the
$16\,384$-dimensional $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$ places a fraction $11\,150/16\,384$ of its energy in
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$, and $\sqrt{11\,150/16\,384}=0.825$. An attacker at
$\varepsilon=\sigma_\text{rel}$ would accordingly have been $1/0.825=1.21$ times
as strong as the noise it is meant to be indistinguishable from, equally at
every noise level. Note also that the noise is added after the measurement rather than to the photon
counts before it, since the post-measurement Gaussian is the
standard idealisation of an inverse problem.\\
Alongside the data, the data-generation stage writes a summary file recording the geometry,
the angle vector, the limited-angle window, the noise level, the mean sinogram
norm, the mean noise norm, the operator norm and the truncation threshold.
Training and attacking both read their configuration from this file rather than
from the command line, which guarantees that the operator used to attack a model
is the operator that generated the data it was trained on.\\
Four noise levels are used, $\sigma_\text{rel}\in\{0.005,\,0.01,\,0.02,\,0.05\}$, each with its own dataset,
its own pair of trained models and its own attack run. Each dataset is split
by index into three parts: the first $3\,500$ samples form the training set,
the next $500$ the validation set and the last $1\,000$ the test set.
The validation set is used for one thing only, choosing
which checkpoint to keep, and the test set for nothing but evaluation. Choosing
the checkpoint on the test set instead would report every result on the very
samples the kept model was selected to do best on, and the clean reconstruction
error in particular would come out optimistically biased.

\subsection{Residual U-Net}\label{sec:training}
Before we can describe the residual U-Net, we need to implement the U-Net itself. We start by defining a double convolution:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=DoubleConv]
class DoubleConv(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels, mid_channels=None):
        super().__init__()
        mid_channels = mid_channels or out_channels
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(in_channels, mid_channels, 3, padding=1, bias=False),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Conv2d(mid_channels, out_channels, 3, padding=1, bias=False),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)
\end{lstlisting}
This applies two convolutions: the first takes the channel count to
$\texttt{mid\_channels}$, the second from there to $\texttt{out\_channels}$.
Left unspecified, the two are equal, so the first convolution sets the channel
count and the second leaves it unchanged.
The expansive path below is the one place where $\texttt{mid\_channels}$ is set explicitly, and it is
the reason the argument exists at all: there the concatenation doubles the
channel count, and the double convolution has to bring it down again in two
steps rather than one. The kernel size is $3$, the padding is $1$, and the convolutions have no bias.
Each convolution is followed by a ReLU activation. The parameter $\texttt{inplace}=\texttt{True}$ makes the activation overwrite its input tensor instead of allocating a new output tensor, which reduces memory consumption.
With this double convolution we can build a downsampling and an upsampling module.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Down and Up]
class Down(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.MaxPool2d(2),
            DoubleConv(in_channels, out_channels),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)

class Up(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels):
        super().__init__()
        self.up = nn.Upsample(scale_factor=2, mode="bilinear",
                              align_corners=True)
        self.conv = DoubleConv(in_channels, out_channels, in_channels // 2)
    def forward(self, x1, x2):
        x1 = self.up(x1)
        diffY, diffX = x2.size(2) - x1.size(2), x2.size(3) - x1.size(3)
        x1 = F.pad(x1, [diffX // 2, diffX - diffX // 2,
                        diffY // 2, diffY - diffY // 2])
        return self.conv(torch.cat([x2, x1], dim=1))
\end{lstlisting}
The downsampling ($\texttt{Down}$) is just the $2\times2$ max pooling of
Section~\ref{sec:unet}, followed by a double convolution which is what raises
the channel count. The upsampling ($\texttt{Up}$) is bilinear upsampling with a
scale factor of $2$, so it doubles the resolution, and it is the counterpart of
that max pooling: pooling keeps one of every four values, bilinear upsampling
restores the resolution by interpolating between the values that survived.
Bilinear here means linear in each of the two image axes separately. A new pixel
falls between four known ones; interpolating linearly along the first axis
between each of the two pairs gives two intermediate values, and interpolating
linearly between those along the second axis gives the new pixel. The result is
a weighted average of the four neighbours whose weights are the products of the
one-dimensional distances. The
parameter $\texttt{align\_corners}=\texttt{True}$ fixes the convention for where
the known samples sit: the corner pixels of input and output are made to
coincide, so the corner values are reproduced exactly and the interpolation grid
is stretched to fit between them.\\
The upsampled tensor is then concatenated with the tensor of the corresponding
downsampling step. This is the skip connection of the original U-Net
\parencite{U-NET}. The concatenation doubles the channel count, and the double
convolution it is passed through undoes that doubling: its intermediate channel
count is set to half its input, which is exactly the
$\texttt{mid\_channels}$ argument of $\texttt{DoubleConv}$. Should the two
tensors disagree in size by a pixel, which happens for odd resolutions, the
upsampled one is padded symmetrically before the concatenation; at our
resolutions, all powers of two, this never triggers.\\
A final $1\times1$ convolution ($\texttt{OutConv}$) reduces the $64$ feature
channels back to the single output channel. A $1\times1$ kernel has no spatial
extent, so it does not mix neighbouring pixels at all. At each pixel
independently it takes the $64$ channel values as a vector and returns one
weighted sum of them, with bias. It is a learned linear combination across
channels, applied identically at every pixel. This collapses a feature stack into one greyscale image.
The U-Net definition is then the following:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=UNET]
class UNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.inc = DoubleConv(1, 64)
        self.down1 = Down(64, 128)
        self.down2 = Down(128, 256)
        self.down3 = Down(256, 512)
        self.down4 = Down(512, 512)
        self.up1 = Up(1024, 256)
        self.up2 = Up(512, 128)
        self.up3 = Up(256, 64)
        self.up4 = Up(128, 64)
        self.outc = OutConv(64, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        x1 = self.inc(x)
        x2 = self.down1(x1)
        x3 = self.down2(x2)
        x4 = self.down3(x3)
        x5 = self.down4(x4)
        x = self.up1(x5, x4)
        x = self.up2(x, x3)
        x = self.up3(x, x2)
        x = self.up4(x, x1)
        return self.outc(x)
\end{lstlisting}
Four downsampling steps take a $128\times128$ input
down to an $8\times8$ bottleneck carrying $512$ channels, and the expansive path
returns it to the original resolution. In total the network has $17\,252\,993$
trainable parameters. The count follows from the shapes alone, since every
parameter sits in a convolution kernel: a $3\times3$ convolution from $c_\text{in}$
to $c_\text{out}$ channels without bias holds $9\,c_\text{in}c_\text{out}$
weights, so a $\texttt{DoubleConv}(c_\text{in},c_\text{out},c_\text{mid})$ holds
$9(c_\text{in}c_\text{mid}+c_\text{mid}c_\text{out})$, and $\texttt{OutConv}$
adds $64\cdot1+1=65$ for its kernel and bias. Summing the ten blocks gives

The two innermost blocks $\texttt{down4}$ and $\texttt{up1}$ hold
$10\,616\,832$ of those, about three fifths of the network, which is the usual
shape of a U-Net: the resolution is lowest at the bottleneck, so channels are
cheapest there in memory and most numerous in parameters.
This gives us an image-to-image network that we can use in both the residual and the Nullspace Network architecture.
The output of the residual network is computed as $\mathbf{x} + \texttt{UNet}(\mathbf{x})$,
which is the reconstruction method $\mathcal{F}_\theta$ of Section~\ref{sec:resunet}
evaluated at $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_\text{init}$.
We now turn to training. The loss function is the mean squared error:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=mse\_loss]
def mse_loss(pred: torch.Tensor, target: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
  """training objective: MSE vs ground truth"""
  return torch.mean((pred - target) ** 2)
\end{lstlisting}
%PersonalTODO: Look into Adam Optimizer
Here the tensor $\texttt{pred}$ is the reconstruction and $\texttt{target}$ is the ground truth. The model parameters are trained with the Adam optimiser \parencite{Adam}, which adapts a per-parameter step size from running estimates of the first and second moments of the gradient. In PyTorch the optimiser is created as
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Adam optimiser]
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.0001)
\end{lstlisting}
In every step the gradient is reset to zero and recomputed by $\texttt{loss.backward()}$.
The optimiser then updates the model parameters. We train for $50$ epochs with a batch size of $32$, without a learning-rate
schedule and without data augmentation.\\
Both are standard devices and both are deliberately omitted. A learning-rate
schedule lowers the step size as training proceeds: a large rate early on to
cross the loss landscape quickly, a small one later to settle into a minimum
instead of bouncing around it. This typically buys a somewhat lower final loss.
Data augmentation enlarges the training set with label-preserving
transformations of it, rotations or flips of the phantom here, and typically
buys a smaller generalisation gap. Neither is used because neither would serve
the question this work asks. Both architectures are trained by the identical
loop, so any accuracy either device bought would be bought for both, while the
comparison of the two is what the results rest on. Moreover, the epoch study of
Section~\ref{sec:epoch} reads attackability against the epoch index, and a
changing learning rate would confound the two: a change along that axis could then
equally well be a change in the step size rather than in the model.\\
Each epoch is followed by an evaluation over the validation set, and whenever
the validation loss improves the weights are written to disk. The checkpoint
with the lowest validation loss is the one attacked and used throughout.\\
Two further artifacts are produced during training, both of them for the epoch
study of Section~\ref{sec:epoch}. Every $N$th epoch a snapshot of the weights is
written, and at the end of training a history file records the training and
validation loss of each epoch together with the index of the best one.\\
The stride $N$ trades resolution along the epoch axis against storage and runtime, and it bounds how precisely the onset of attackability can be located:
at stride $N$, the epoch at which a curve turns is known only to within $N$ epochs.
Since that epoch is what the study is asking for, all experiments use $N=1$ and every epoch is both stored and attacked.
One snapshot of $17.3$ million single-precision parameters is $69$\,MB, so $N=1$ costs
$3.4$\,GB per model and noise level, $28$\,GB over the two architectures and
four noise levels. Because the epoch study attacks every snapshot it
writes, the same factor reappears in its runtime. That is the price of locating the epoch at which a model becomes attackable to a single epoch.
The history file is what lets the study plot attackability against the loss curves,
so it is always written regardless of $N$. Since the two architectures differ only in the
wrapper around the U-Net, one training loop serves both.

\subsection{Nullspace Network}\label{sec:nsn-impl}
The Nullspace Network uses exactly the U-Net of the previous section; the two
architectures differ only in how the correction of the U-Net is applied.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The two wrappers]
class RESNET(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.unet(x)

class NSN(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.radon.proj_null_image(self.unet(x))
\end{lstlisting}
Here $\texttt{proj\_null\_image}$ is the operator
$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:pinv}, so the second wrapper is exactly the reconstruction map
$\mathbf{x}_\text{out}=\mathbf{x}_\text{init}+
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$ of
Section~\ref{sec:nsn}.\\
The projection sits inside the computational graph and not after it.
At inference this would make no difference to the form of the map, since projecting the correction of a
trained residual network would also give a data-consistent map. During training, however,
it matters, and the reason is the chain rule. Write $\mathbf{r}=N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$
for the raw network output, so that the reconstruction is
$\mathbf{x}_\text{out}=\mathbf{x}_\text{init}+\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{r}$.
The loss is the training objective of the previous section, the mean squared error of the
reconstruction against the ground truth,
\[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_\text{out})=\frac{1}{n}\lVert\mathbf{x}_\text{out}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2^2,
\qquad
\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}_\text{out}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{2}{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_\text{out}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\right)
=\frac{2}{n}\,\mathbf{e},\]
that is, up to the factor $2/n$, the reconstruction error itself. Differentiating it through
the last linear step of the forward pass transposes the operator,
\[\nabla_{\!\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{L}
=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{*}\,\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}_\text{out}}\mathcal{L}
=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}_\text{out}}\mathcal{L}
=\frac{2}{n}\,\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}},\]
the first equality by the chain rule, since $\mathbf{x}_\text{out}$ depends on $\mathbf{r}$
only through the linear map $\mathbf{r}\mapsto\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{r}$,
whose Jacobian is the projector itself, and differentiating backwards through a linear map
applies its adjoint; the second equality because an orthogonal projector is self-adjoint,
$\mathbf{P}^{*}=\mathbf{P}$; and the third by the definition of the error decomposition of
Section~\ref{sec:pinv}. What reaches the U-Net output is therefore the null-space component of
the error alone, itself a null-space vector: the range floor is not merely uncorrectable at inference,
it is invisible to training. Whatever the loss would like to change
about the component of $\mathbf{r}$ in ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{\perp}$, no
gradient signal to that effect ever reaches the weights.
The Nullspace Network is thus never rewarded for producing a correction in ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{\perp}$,
which would be discarded anyway. The capacity that a post-hoc variant would spend on learning such a correction
is, in our architecture, spent entirely inside $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$.\\
The realisation of the properties claimed in
Section~\ref{sec:nsn} is checked numerically against the real operator.
The test suite verifies that $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ is
idempotent, that the error decomposition reproduces the error exactly and splits
it orthogonally, and that the correction of a Nullspace Network is invisible to
the measurements. The data-consistency check is not
$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$. Under truncation it
is false: $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$
retains the discarded small-singular-value directions, and $\mathbf{A}$
maps those to something small but nonzero. What does vanish exactly, and what the
test asserts, is the \emph{measured} trace,
\[\mathbf{P}_k\,\mathbf{A}\,
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0,\]
which is the identity the reconstruction actually relies on, because
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ is the only part of sinogram space anything in this work
ever occupies. The clean measurement enters the reconstruction only through
$\mathbf{A}^{+}=\mathbf{V}_k\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$, which reads its argument through
$\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$ and is therefore blind to everything else; the simulated noise of
Section~\ref{sec:data} is drawn there by construction; the adversarial perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:adversarial} is confined there by its feasible set; and the data-consistency residual
of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} projects there before comparing. A discrepancy outside
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ is one no quantity in this work can observe, which is why
the exact statement, and not the textbook $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$,
is the one worth asserting. One further
point: the check is made with untrained weights and holds for any weights at
all, because the property is a property of the architecture and not of what the
network has learned.

\subsection{Adversarial attacks}\label{sec:adversarial}
The attack differentiates the whole chain from the sinogram to the reconstruction,
\[\mathbf{y}\;\longmapsto\;\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}
\;\longmapsto\;\mathbf{x}_\text{init}=\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}
\;\longmapsto\;\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}),\]
where $\mathbf{P}_k=\mathbf{U}_k\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$
is the orthogonal projector onto the retained range of the limited-angle
operator and the last arrow is the wrapper of the respective architecture,
$\mathbf{x}_\text{init}+N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$ or
$\mathbf{x}_\text{init}+\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$.
The attack code is independent of the model: it needs only a single adapter, which applies
$\mathbf{P}_k$ and $\mathbf{A}^{+}$, and the wrapper around the neural network.
This way neither the budget, the step size, the starting points nor the projection can differ
between the two architectures. Any difference in the reported numbers is a difference of architecture.\\
The first arrow also applies to the clean sinogram before the attack begins:
what is attacked is $\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta$
rather than the $\mathbf{y}^\delta$ that was written to disk. This changes
nothing about the network input, because
$\mathbf{A}^+=\mathbf{V}_k\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$ annihilates exactly
the component the projection removes, so
$\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{y}^\delta=\mathbf{A}^+
\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta$ identically.
It is worth being explicit that this does not hand the attacker any advantage, because at first
sight the step looks as if it might. The projection removes a component that $\mathbf{A}^{+}$ would
have annihilated anyway. Both models are fed exactly the reconstruction they would have been
fed without it, and the perturbation is still constrained to the same set and the same radius,
so nothing about the attack is made easier. What it does change is the denominator of the
reported relative perturbation, which is measured against
$\lVert\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta\rVert_2$ rather than
$\lVert\mathbf{y}^\delta\rVert_2$. That is the honest choice of the two. The discarded
component is one no reconstruction in this work responds to, and leaving it in the denominator
would make the attack look smaller relative to a signal that is partly invisible to the
model.\\\\
\textbf{Budget.} The budget is relative and per sample,
$\varepsilon_i=\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{y}_i\rVert_2$ with $\mathbf{y}_i$ the projected sinogram $\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta_i$ of sample $i$, so a bright and
a faint sinogram are attacked at the same relative strength. Unless stated
otherwise $\varepsilon$ is set to $\sigma_\text{rel}$, the relative noise level
of Section~\ref{sec:data} for the dataset the model was trained on, so that the
attacker is granted exactly as much energy as the
measurement noise the model has already learned to tolerate, and is made to spend it in the
same subspace the noise occupies. A perturbation that
does real damage within that budget is therefore a perturbation indistinguishable in size
from noise the scanner produces anyway. Keeping that equivalence exact is what the subspace
argument of Section~\ref{sec:data} and the feasible set below are for.\\\\
\textbf{Step size and restarts.} The step size is derived from the budget, per
sample and in the same units,
\[\alpha_i = \frac{2.5\,\varepsilon_i}{T},\qquad T=50,\]
which is the $2.5\,\varepsilon/T$ step size customarily used with PGD\@.
The rule comes from the evaluation protocol of the robustness library of Madry's group,
which evaluates every model at a step size of $2.5\,\varepsilon/T$ so that the
setting does not have to be retuned whenever the budget or the iteration count changes
\parencite{robustness}. The factor $2.5$ gives the iteration a total travel of
$2.5\varepsilon_i$, more than the diameter $2\varepsilon_i$ of the ball, so the iterate can reach
the boundary from any starting point and still has steps left to refine its direction there;
a factor near $1$ would only just reach the boundary, and a much larger one would spend every
step being projected back. Tying $\alpha_i$ to $\varepsilon_i$ rather than to a
dataset-wide mean matters because the budget is itself per sample. A faint
sinogram and a bright one are then stepped at the same fraction of their own
budget, so the number of effective iterations does not depend on how bright the
phantom happens to be.\\
Each attack is run from $2$ independent random starting points. A starting point
is drawn as a random direction in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$,
$\mathbf{P}_k$ applied to a standard normal vector. This is uniform on the directions of that
subspace and not merely supported on it, which is what the restarts need. A standard normal
vector is invariant in distribution under every orthogonal map of the ambient space, so its
image under $\mathbf{P}_k$, written in an orthonormal basis of
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$, is a standard normal vector of $\mathbb{R}^{k}$; a
Gaussian with covariance a multiple of the identity has no preferred direction, so after
normalisation the draw is uniform on the unit sphere of the subspace.
The draw is then normalised to unit length and scaled to a radius drawn uniformly from
$[0,\varepsilon_i]$, so that it is a random point of the feasible set $\mathcal{C}$ in the
sense of Section~\ref{sec:attacks}. The better one of the two restarts is kept, by comparing the
objective of each sample separately.\\\\
\textbf{Feasible set.} Writing $\mathbf{P}$ for
$\mathbf{P}_k$, the projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:attacks} is implemented as
\[\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})=\mathbf{P}\,
\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\delta}\right),
\qquad
\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}\cdot
\min\!\left(1,\frac{\varepsilon}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_2}\right),\]
with the ball projection applied per sample.\\
The outer $\mathbf{P}$ is, mathematically, redundant. $\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}$
multiplies each sample by a scalar, and scaling a vector cannot take it out of a
subspace, so $\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\delta})$ already lies in
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ and the second $\mathbf{P}$ changes
nothing. It is applied anyway for a numerical reason: the iterate is projected
and rescaled once per step for $50$ steps in single precision, and without the
re-projection the small component that rounding introduces outside the subspace
accumulates over those steps. The cost is one additional pair of matrix products
per step against an attack that already pays several, and it keeps the
invariant the test suite asserts true to single-precision tolerance rather than approximately.
That is, that the returned $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ satisfies $\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}$.\\
The feasible set of our attacks is therefore
\[\mathcal{C}:=\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)\cap B_\varepsilon,\]
the same set the simulated measurement noise of Section~\ref{sec:data} is drawn
from, and a refinement of the $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})\cap B_\varepsilon$ of
Section~\ref{sec:attacks}. That the two sets coincide is what the choice
$\varepsilon=\sigma_\text{rel}$ above rests on. Without it the budget and the noise level would
be equal as numbers while describing perturbations of different size.\\
Restricting the attacker to $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$ rather than to
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$ costs nothing in strength. Energy placed in
the discarded directions is annihilated by $\mathbf{A}^{+}$ before it reaches the
network, so it could only be budget the attacker wastes.\\
With these pieces the iteration of Section~\ref{sec:attacks}, restarts included, takes fewer
than twenty lines of code. In the listing below, $\texttt{random\_start}$ draws the starting point
described above, $\texttt{project\_delta}$ is the projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ given above, and
$\texttt{adapter.projector}$ is $\mathbf{P}$.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The PGD loop]
for _ in range(SUITE_RESTARTS):
    delta = random_start(y_clean, eps, adapter.projector)


    for _ in range(SUITE_STEPS):
        y_adv = (y_clean + delta).detach().requires_grad_(True)
        pred, _, _ = adapter.forward(y_adv, project=False)
        grad = torch.autograd.grad(loss_of(pred), y_adv)[0]
        with torch.no_grad():
            g = grad / l2_norm_batch(grad).clamp_min(1e-12).view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
            delta = project_delta(delta + alpha * g, eps, adapter.projector)
    with torch.no_grad():  # keep the better restart, per sample
        pred, _, _ = adapter.forward(y_clean + delta, project=False)
        score = loss_of(pred, reduction="none")
        better = score > best_score
        best_score = torch.where(better, score, best_score)
        best_delta = torch.where(better.view(-1, 1, 1, 1), delta, best_delta)
\end{lstlisting}
Three details of the loop correspond to the derivation. The gradient is
taken with respect to the perturbed sinogram $\texttt{y\_adv}$ rather than the
perturbation, which is the same gradient because the two differ by the constant
$\mathbf{y}$, and it is the form autograd needs.
As described at the start of Section~\ref{sec:implementation}, PyTorch records each operation as a node of a
graph. The leaves of that graph are the tensors which were not themselves computed from
anything recorded, and a backward pass accumulates gradients at the leaves and nowhere else;
an interior node passes its gradient on rather than keeping it. A tensor becomes a leaf by
being detached, which discards the record of how it was produced, and it is differentiated
with respect to only if it is additionally marked $\texttt{requires\_grad}$. The line
$\texttt{y\_adv = (y\_clean + delta).detach().requires\_grad\_(True)}$ does both, and it is
why the loop rebuilds $\texttt{y\_adv}$ at every step instead of carrying $\texttt{delta}$
through the graph from one iteration to the next. Carrying it would have two costs. The graph
would grow by one full forward pass per step, since each iterate would still remember the
fifty that produced it, and the gradient returned would be the gradient through all of them
rather than the one the derivation calls for, namely the gradient of the loss at the current
iterate. Detaching cuts that history, and the update that follows is performed under
$\texttt{torch.no\_grad()}$ for the same reason. The projection and the step are the
optimiser's own arithmetic and have no business appearing in the next graph. The step is added, so that the
loss is maximised. And the gradient is normalised in the $\ell_2$ norm before the step,
exactly as derived in Section~\ref{sec:attacks}. The model is held in evaluation mode
throughout and gradients are requested only with respect to the input, so no
weight is ever modified by an attack.\\
The final block of the loop selects the restart. After the last step of a restart the objective
is evaluated once more, per sample rather than as a batch mean, and every sample keeps the
perturbation of whichever restart scored higher on it. Choosing one restart for the whole batch
would hand every sample the restart that is best on average, which is weaker for any sample whose
own best came from the other restart, and would understate how attackable the model is.\\
Five attacks are run over every model, differing only in the loss that
$\texttt{loss\_of}$ evaluates: the untargeted attack on the total error, the two
subspace attacks of the next section, and the two targeted attacks of the one
after that.
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks on Subspaces}
The two subspace losses of Section~\ref{sec:subspace} are direct transcriptions of their
definitions.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The subspace objectives]
if objective == "null":
    err = pred - x_gt
    return reduce_loss(radon.proj_null_image(err) ** 2)

if objective == "range":
    err = pred - x_gt
    return reduce_loss((err - radon.proj_null_image(err)) ** 2)
\end{lstlisting}
Up to the constant factor by which a mean differs from a squared norm, the first is $\mathcal{L}_\mathcal{N}$ and the second
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$; a constant factor does not affect the direction of the gradient.
Both use the same projector $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ as the
Nullspace Network itself, and for an attack on that network it therefore occurs
twice in the graph, once inside the model, where it constrains the correction,
and once inside the loss, where it selects the component being maximised, and it is differentiated in both places.
For an attack on the residual network it occurs only in the loss.\\
The layout choice matters here more than anywhere else, and the precision with it. The GA102
processor of the A6000 carries two double-precision units per streaming multiprocessor against
$128$ single-precision ones, so its double-precision throughput is a sixty-fourth of its
single-precision throughput \parencite{GA102}. Running these projections in double precision
would therefore not cost the factor of two that the doubled memory suggests but something
closer to two orders of magnitude, and it would do so at every one of the four applications
per step described below. This is what the argument of Section~\ref{sec:pinv} buys, and it is
why it was worth making.
Applying the projector once costs two matrix products against $\mathbf{V}_k$, namely
$\mathbf{V}_k^{*}\mathbf{v}$ followed by $\mathbf{V}_k(\cdot)$, that is
$2kn\approx3.7\cdot10^{8}$ multiply-accumulates per sample, where
$k=11\,150$ is the number of retained singular directions and $n=16\,384$ the
number of pixels. Against the Nullspace Network the attack pays that four times
per step, twice in the forward pass and twice again in the backward pass,
for $50$ iterations and $2$ restarts on every sample. It remains affordable
because these are dense single-precision products on the GPU, which is what the
layout choice of Section~\ref{sec:pinv} was made for. 
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks using Examples}
The two targeted losses of Section~\ref{sec:targeted} are negated squared distances, so that
maximising them moves the reconstruction towards the target.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The targeted objectives]
if objective == "zero":
    return -reduce_loss(pred ** 2)

if objective == "target":
    return -reduce_loss((pred - target.detach()) ** 2)
\end{lstlisting}
The first realises $\mathbf{t}=0$ and needs no reference image at all. The
second does, and it is supplied once per batch: the target of each sample is the
ground truth of a different sample of the same batch, so no extra data is needed
and the targets are as plausible as the phantoms themselves.\\
The targets are assigned by a derangement of the batch indices, a permutation
that leaves no index in place. It is drawn by rejection. A random permutation is
sampled and redrawn if it fixes any index, with a cyclic shift used as a
fallback in the rare case that twenty draws all fail. The point is that no sample
may be steered towards its own ground truth, which would turn the targeted
attack into no attack at all for that sample and would quietly weaken the
reported numbers.\\
\subsubsection{Epoch study}\label{sec:epoch}
The epoch study investigates whether there is a specific point during training at which a model becomes attackable,
and in particular whether attackability coincides with the onset of overfitting.
It consumes the epoch snapshots written by the training loop of Section~\ref{sec:training}. For each architecture,
every snapshot is loaded in turn, attacked with the untargeted
squared-error objective at a fixed budget of
$\varepsilon=0.01$, and evaluated.
This budget is deliberately not the one the attacks of Section~\ref{sec:adversarial} use.
The question here is at which epoch a model
becomes attackable, and it is asked of both architectures at each of the four noise levels,
so in total of eight models.
Holding $\varepsilon$ fixed keeps the attacker identical across all of them, so the curves
can be read against one another and a difference between two of them is a difference between
the models rather than between the budgets they were probed with. The cost of the choice
should be stated: at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.05$ the attacker is granted a fifth of the noise the
model tolerates and at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.005$ twice as much, so the vertical position of a
curve is not comparable across noise levels, only its shape along the epoch axis is.
The study writes its own table, one row per snapshot,
carrying the adversarial error of that snapshot next to
the training and validation loss which it reads back from the history file of
Section~\ref{sec:training}. The two quantities therefore end up on one axis
without the training loop and the attack having to know anything about each
other. Because the study repeats a full attack once per snapshot, it runs on $32$
test samples rather than the $128$ used for the other attacks.
At $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$ the study is repeated with the objective restricted to the null space.
We need to do this, since otherwise the attackability we investigate is that of the pseudoinverse and not that of the model.\\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
The inputs are cached once and every snapshot is attacked on that same cache.
This matters for reading the resulting curve. The phantoms, their sinograms and
their initial reconstructions are byte-identical from epoch to epoch, so the
only quantity that varies along the curve is the weights, and any trend in it is
a trend in the model rather than in the sample of test data it was measured on.

\subsection{Metrics and error decomposition}\label{sec:metrics}
Every attacked sample is scored with the same set of seven metrics, each
computed between a reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and the ground truth
$\mathbf{x}^\dagger$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Relative $\ell_2$ error},
$\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2/
\lVert\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2$. Its
square is the quantity the error decomposition below is additive in,
the numerator splits as
$\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}\rVert^2+
\lVert\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}\rVert^2$ and the denominator is common to both
terms. The normalisation
$\lVert\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2$ makes it comparable across phantoms of
different brightness.
The denominator is bounded from below by $10^{-3}\sqrt{n}$ to prevent a blow-up on near-empty phantoms.
\item \textbf{RMSE}, $\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}({(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger)}^2)}$,
the same error unnormalised, in image units.
\item \textbf{NRMSE}, the RMSE divided by the data range
$\max\mathbf{x}^\dagger-\min\mathbf{x}^\dagger$. A normalisation by
contrast rather than by energy.
\item \textbf{PSNR}, $20\log_{10}(\text{range})-10\log_{10}(\text{MSE})$ in
decibels. The same information as the RMSE on a logarithmic scale.
\item \textbf{SSIM}, structural similarity \parencite{SSIM}. It compares local means, variances
and covariances, so it responds to structure being moved or invented rather than
merely to intensity being wrong.
\item \textbf{MAE}, $\operatorname{mean}\lvert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rvert$.
Less sensitive to a few large deviations than the squared metrics, so a gap
between MAE and RMSE indicates that the error is concentrated rather than spread.
\item \textbf{Maximum absolute error},
$\max\lvert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rvert$. It
sees only the single worst pixel. A small mean error with a large maximum is a
localised artefact, which is the clinically relevant kind.
\end{itemize}
All seven are recorded for the clean and for the adversarial reconstruction, and
separately for the initial reconstruction. The relative
$\ell_2$ error, PSNR, SSIM and MAE are recorded separately for the initial reconstruction as well,
so that the damage the attack does to the network input can be told apart from the damage it does
to the output.
Reports include the mean with a $95\%$ confidence interval of half-width
$1.96\,s/\sqrt{N}$, where $s$ is the sample standard deviation of the metric over
the $N$ attacked samples and $1.96$ the normal quantile. They also include the median
and the quartiles. The interval is reported because the headline comparison is a
comparison of two means over $N=128$ samples, and a difference that the two intervals
can account for cannot be told apart from sampling error. The interval quantifies the sampling error of
this particular test set, not the variability of the attack. The median and
quartiles are reported alongside because the distribution over samples can be
skewed.\\
In addition, every error field is decomposed as in Section~\ref{sec:pinv} into
$\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$, and the norms of
both components as well as their fractions of $\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert$ are
recorded. Since only the squared $\ell_2$ norm is additive over this
decomposition, the remaining metrics cannot be split the same way.
Instead the component-only images $\mathbf{x}^\dagger+\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$ and
$\mathbf{x}^\dagger+\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}$ are scored with the same metrics.
This shows how much of the loss in a metric each subspace is responsible for
on its own. These are not reconstructions any method produced, they are the ground truth with one component
of an error added back. The two numbers do not add up to the number for the
full reconstruction, because SSIM and PSNR are not additive over the
decomposition, which is precisely why this construction is needed in the first place.
They should be read as an attribution of the damage, not as a decomposition of the score.\\
Data consistency asks whether a reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, measured again, reproduces
a given sinogram $\mathbf{y}$. We measure it by the relative residual
\[r(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{P}_k
\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2}
{\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2}.\]
The projection $\mathbf{P}_k$ is there because the sinograms the networks receive are
projected onto $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$, while $\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ need not be.
Without it the residual would contain a part that no input could ever match. Note that
$\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_k\Sigma_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$, so the residual only compares
the $k$ directions that are actually used for the reconstruction.\\
We record the residual against two sinograms. Against the sinogram the network was actually given,
$\mathbf{P}_k(\mathbf{y}^\delta+\boldsymbol{\delta})$, it is data consistency in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:nsn}. Against the unattacked sinogram $\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta$ it measures
agreement with what the scanner actually measured. Since the two sinograms differ only by
$\mathbf{P}_k\boldsymbol{\delta}$, the triangle inequality gives
\[\big|\,\lVert\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{P}_k(\mathbf{y}^\delta+\boldsymbol{\delta})\rVert_2
-\lVert\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta\rVert_2\,\big|
\;\leq\;\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_2 ,\]
so after normalisation the two residuals differ by at most about $\varepsilon$. This bounds
only their difference, not the residuals themselves. A reconstruction that fits neither sinogram
has two large residuals, which are simply close to each other. For the Nullspace Network both values are known in advance. Since
$\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$, it reproduces exactly the sinogram
it was given, so the first residual is zero up to rounding, whatever the attack does. The second is
then just the size of the perturbation,
\[r(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta)
=\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_2}{\lVert\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta\rVert_2}
\;\leq\;\varepsilon .\]
For the Residual U-Net nothing ties the output to the data, so both residuals have to be measured.\\
\subsection{Lipschitz Study}\label{sec:lipschitz}
Finally, an attack-independent robustness measure is computed. Every number
above is the outcome of one particular attack, so none of them can distinguish a
robust model from one the attack merely failed to find a direction for. Write
$f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}$ for the image-to-image map the wrapper implements,
so $f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+N_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ for the residual network and
$f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x})$
for the Nullspace Network, and let $g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}$ be
the learned correction alone. Linearising $g$ around the clean initial
reconstruction $\mathbf{x}_0$ and restricting both its input and its output to
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ with the same projector gives
\[\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,
\mathbf{J}_g(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})},\]
whose largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{T})$ is the local gain of the
learned channel, that is, the factor by which a worst-case null-space perturbation of the
input can be amplified into null-space error at the output. A value below one
means the correction contracts in that subspace, above one that it amplifies.\\
$\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{T})$ is estimated by power iteration and the Jacobian is never assembled. One
power-iteration step needs $\mathbf{T}\mathbf{d}$ and
$\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{u}$, which are exactly a Jacobian-vector and a
vector-Jacobian product of $g$ with a projection on each side, and PyTorch
supplies both directly:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=One power iteration of the restricted gain]
proj = image_projector(radon, restriction)   # N(A), its complement, or identity

def G(x):                      # learned correction, restricted by proj
    return proj(model(x) - x)

d = proj(torch.randn_like(x0))
d = d / torch.linalg.norm(d.reshape(-1))
for _ in range(LIPSCHITZ_ITERS):
    _, u = torch.autograd.functional.jvp(G, x0, d, strict=False)
    _, w = torch.autograd.functional.vjp(G, x0, proj(u), strict=False)
    w = proj(w)
    d = w / torch.linalg.norm(w.reshape(-1))
_, u = torch.autograd.functional.jvp(G, x0, d, strict=False)
sigma = torch.linalg.norm(proj(u).reshape(-1))
\end{lstlisting}
Two features distinguish this from a plain Lipschitz estimate of the network.
It is local: it is the gain of the linearisation at one point
$\mathbf{x}_0$, not a global bound over all inputs, so it describes the
neighbourhood the reconstruction actually lives in rather than a worst case over
image space. This makes it comparable to an attack that also starts
there. Second it is restricted: input and output are both confined to
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$. That restriction is what makes the number comparable
between the two architectures at all. Unrestricted, the Nullspace Network's
correction is identically zero on ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{\perp}$ while the residual network's is not,
so an unrestricted estimate can not be used to fairly compare the Nullspace Network and the residual U-Net.
Instead we need to measure them on the subspace both are free to act in, this allows us to compare the learned maps.\\
The same power iteration is run with the projector replaced by
$\mathbf{P}_{{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{\perp}}$ and by the identity.
This gives more insight into the general behaviour of the residual network:
the complement-restricted gain is zero for the Nullspace Network by construction, and its value for the residual network is
precisely the part of an unrestricted comparison that would be architecture rather
than learning. The unrestricted gain is the plain local Lipschitz constant of the
learned correction, reported for completeness.\\
The null-restricted gain has one limitation of its own. A perturbation of the measurement reaches
the network only through the initial reconstruction, which it changes by
$\mathbf{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{\delta}\in{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^\perp$, never by a vector in
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$. The null-restricted gain therefore describes the response to perturbations
no attacker can make. The gain an attack on the null-space component exploits is the cross gain
\[\big\lVert\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,\mathbf{J}_g(\mathbf{x}_0)\,
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})\big\rVert_2\]
from the measured part of the input into the unmeasured part of the output. It is estimated by the
same power iteration, with $\mathbf{P}_{{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{\perp}}$ applied to the input and
$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ to the output. Like the null-restricted gain it compares the
learned maps, since both networks are free to map a measured input perturbation into the null space.
For the Nullspace Network, whose correction lies in $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$, it is the whole
response of the correction to input perturbations in ${\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{\perp}$.
Each of the four gains is estimated with $16$ power iterations at each of the first $32$ clean initial
reconstructions of the attacked test samples, and the maximum over these $32$ points is reported
alongside the mean.

\subsection{Experimental pipeline}
Computation and rendering are kept strictly apart. An attack run writes only
numeric artifacts: the per-sample metrics as a table, the adversarial sinograms
and perturbations, the example images with their per-sample metrics, the
cross-model transfer stacks and the aggregated summary. Every figure in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is produced afterwards from those files alone, without a model, a checkpoint or
a Radon operator being loaded. A figure can therefore be redrawn at any time
without repeating the attack that produced it, which matters because a full
attack run takes several GPU-hours.\\
One noise level is one Slurm job. The script $\texttt{slurm\_full\_run.sh}$ runs the whole
experiment for that level in sequence: test suite, truncation study, data generation, training,
the attack suite together with the Lipschitz estimate, the epoch study, and finally the rendering.
Every stage aborts the job on failure, since each consumes the output of the ones
before it. The truncation study is the one exception: unless the run takes its $\tau$ from it, nothing
downstream reads it, so a failure there is reported and the job continues. Individual stages can be skipped by environment variable, which is what makes it
possible to re-attack or re-render without regenerating data or retraining. The four noise
levels are entirely independent of one another and are submitted as four such jobs, so they
run concurrently if the cluster allows it and the critical path is one noise level deep
rather than four.\\
Reproducibility rests on three things: fixed seeds, one cached set of attack inputs shared by
all models, and a test suite run against the real operator.\\
The random seeds are fixed for data generation, for training and for the attacks, so rerunning
a stage reproduces the inputs exactly. The phantoms are drawn from DIVAL's fixed seeds.
Only training is not reproducible bit for bit, because some of the convolution kernels cuDNN uses
on the GPU are not deterministic; two training runs therefore agree closely but not exactly.
All models within a run are then attacked on one cached set of inputs, so that
they see identical data.\\
The identities the argument of this thesis depends on are covered by a test suite that
exercises them against the real operator rather than a mock: the idempotence of the null-space
projector, the vanishing of its measured trace
$\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$, the agreement of the truncated
factors with the sparse system matrix, the orthogonality of the error decomposition and the
enforcement of the attack budget, together with the per-sample choice of restart and the
integrity of the geometry cache. The factors themselves are checked at every build and every
load, as described in Section~\ref{sec:pinv}.

Judged by their total errors, the two architectures are far apart. The residual network reduces the
relative $\ell_2$ error of the pseudoinverse by a factor of about twenty at every noise level and keeps
an SSIM above $0.9$. The Nullspace Network reduces it far less, and at $\sigma_{\text{rel}}=0.05$
hardly at all. The reason is the constraint of the Nullspace Network: the range component of its
error is exactly that of the pseudoinverse, to every digit printed in Table~\ref{tab:clean}, because
the two are the same quantity. With
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}_0+\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ the
correction has no component outside $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$, so
\[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger)
=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})(\mathbf{x}_0-\mathbf{x}^\dagger)\]
identically. Measured per sample, the ratio
$\lVert\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}\rVert/\lVert\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}^{\,\text{init}}\rVert$
lies within $5\cdot10^{-7}$ of $1$ for the Nullspace Network in all nine runs of this work and under
all five attacks, clean and attacked alike. This is what the identity above predicts, and it confirms
numerically that the implementation meets its hypotheses and that the Nullspace Network is data consistent.
The unconstrained residual network removes more than $90\%$ of the range component, which is what gives it the far smaller total error.\\
The null-space component of the pseudoinverse error, $0.2736$, is the same at all four noise levels.
The reason is that $\mathbf{x}_\text{init}=\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{y}^\delta$ lies in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{V}_k)$, so
$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}(\mathbf{x}_\text{init}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger)=-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{x}^\dagger$:
this component is the part of the phantom the truncated operator never measures, and the noise cannot reach it.
The four datasets share their phantoms, so the value is not merely the same in expectation but identical to every digit printed.\\
On the channel the Nullspace Network does control, the picture reverses. Its clean null-space error
is lower than that of the residual network at every noise level, by up to a factor of two at the highest.
Restricted to the component it can correct, the Nullspace Network outperforms the unconstrained residual network;
it spends all of its capacity there, while the residual network also has to correct the range component.
Every total error of the Nullspace Network in Table~\ref{tab:clean}
is the inherited floor on the range component plus a small learned term.
A comparison of totals between the two architectures is consequently a comparison of the constraint, not of the learned
maps, which is why we focus on the error decomposition from here on.

\subsubsection{Data consistency}\label{sec:results-consistency}
Figure~\ref{fig:consistency} shows the data-consistency residual $r(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{y})$ of
Section~\ref{sec:metrics} against the sinogram each reconstruction was computed from, at
$\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$. For the Nullspace Network it is zero to rounding, clean and attacked
alike, as $\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$ demands. Its median is of the
order of $10^{-6}$, the level of single-precision rounding, so its bars are too small to be visible.
The residual U-Net has a clean median residual of $0.012$, close to the noise level. This is what a
good reconstruction should have. We can see this by assuming $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ to be the ground truth itself. Its residual
against the noisy sinogram would then be
$\lVert\mathbf{P}_k(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^\dagger-\mathbf{y}^\delta)\rVert_2/\lVert\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta\rVert_2$,
the relative size of the noise, which is about $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$. A residual near the noise
level therefore means that the residual network does not reproduce the noise, whereas the Nullspace
Network, whose range component is that of $\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{y}^\delta$, reproduces it exactly.\\
Under the untargeted attack the residual of the residual U-Net rises almost eightfold, to nine times
the noise level. By the triangle inequality of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} its residual
against the unattacked sinogram lies within about $\varepsilon=0.01$ of this value, so the attacked
reconstruction explains neither sinogram. The other noise levels behave alike: the clean residual
stays close to the noise level, and the attack raises it four- to elevenfold.

%is not equally clear everywhere: on the multi-ellipse phantoms of Section~\ref{sec:results-data} the

Data consistency certifies that a reconstruction explains its data,
not that these data are the ones the scanner produced, and it says nothing about the null-space
component of the image, which the measurement never constrained. That component is what the
attacks of the following sections target.

\paragraph{The untargeted attack.} Against the residual U-Net, the untargeted attack raises the
median relative error by a factor of about $22$, at a perturbation as large as the noise the network
was trained on (Table~\ref{tab:attacks}). Most of the damage is done in the null space: the
null-space component of the error grows thirtyfold, the range component fourteenfold
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomp-mse}). The attack thus makes the residual network produce structure that
the measurements cannot see.\\
Against the Nullspace Network the same attack more than quadruples the total error, which taken at
face value would make the Nullspace Network the far more vulnerable of the two. The decomposition
shows otherwise: practically the entire attacked error is range component, while the null-space
component stays small. This is the reason for the subspace attacks of
Section~\ref{sec:subspace}: whatever the network does, the range component of its error moves by
$\mathbf{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{\delta}$ and by nothing else, and an attack on the total error finds it
cheapest to grow that component.\\
Due to the reconstruction with the pseudoinverse, the growth of the range component of the error is
bounded by $\varepsilon(1+\sigma_\text{rel})/\tau$ in units of $\lVert\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2$, since
$\lVert\mathbf{A}^{+}\rVert_2\leq1/(\tau\sigma_{\max})$. The largest observed growth reaches two thirds
of this bound and the median $38\%$ of it (Table~\ref{tab:prop}). The gap has two sources. The attack
realises about $85\%$ of the worst case $\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_2/\sigma_{\min}$ of each sample,
and a single-ellipse sinogram has only about $45\%$ of the norm
$(1+\sigma_\text{rel})\,\sigma_{\max}\lVert\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2$ the bound allows for. Neither
factor depends on the noise level, and the budget
$\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{P}_k\mathbf{y}^\delta\rVert_2$ of Section~\ref{sec:adversarial} is
proportional to $\varepsilon$, so at all four noise levels the median growth is the same multiple of
the budget, $96\,\varepsilon$, as befits a component that depends on the perturbation only
through the linear map $\mathbf{A}^{+}$. The untargeted attack against the Nullspace Network thus
does not measure the network, but the pseudoinverse. A comparison of total adversarial errors between the
two architectures is for this reason as meaningless as the comparison of clean totals in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, and the remainder of this section compares the architectures on the null-space
component alone.

\subsection{Targeted adversarial attack}\label{sec:results-targeted}
In Figure~\ref{fig:channels} the samples of the two targeted attacks, towards the zero image and
towards the ground truth of another sample, form the cluster closest to the origin for both
networks. Their damage is the smallest of all five attacks. This is expected, since reaching one
particular image is harder than leaving the ground truth in any direction. The medians of
%TODO: CHeck math of followoing paragraph
Table~\ref{tab:attacks} confirm this: for the residual network the two targeted attacks reach about
half the error of the untargeted attack.\\
For the Nullspace Network the total error hardly moves at all: the attack towards the zero image
leaves it practically unchanged, and the attack towards another sample raises it by a tenth. The
decomposition shows why. Both attacks raise the null-space error of the Nullspace Network about
25-fold, but they lower its range error, by a quarter and by an eighth.\\
The reason is the way the pseudoinverse weights the measurement. The range component of the
reconstruction is $\mathbf{A}^{+}(\mathbf{y}^\delta+\boldsymbol{\delta})$, and its coefficient along
$\mathbf{v}_i$ is
\[\mathbf{v}_i^{*}\mathbf{x}^\dagger+\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^{*}(\mathbf{y}^\delta-\mathbf{y})}{\sigma_i}
+\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^{*}\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\sigma_i},\]
the coefficient of the phantom, the amplified noise and the amplified perturbation. A targeted attack
minimises the squared distance of the reconstruction to its target $\mathbf{t}$, whose range part is
the sum of the squared residuals $r_i$ of these coefficients against $\mathbf{v}_i^{*}\mathbf{t}$.
Since $r_i$ depends on the perturbation through $\mathbf{u}_i^{*}\boldsymbol{\delta}/\sigma_i$, the
derivative of $r_i^2$ with respect to $\mathbf{u}_i^{*}\boldsymbol{\delta}$ is $2r_i/\sigma_i$: to first
order, a unit of budget spent along $\mathbf{u}_i$ reduces the range part of the squared distance by
$2\lvert r_i\rvert/\sigma_i$. The noise is spread evenly over the directions $\mathbf{u}_i$, while the
share $\sigma_i\mathbf{v}_i^{*}\mathbf{x}^\dagger$ of the phantom in the measurement shrinks with
$\sigma_i$, so at the smallest retained singular values the amplified noise dominates the coefficient,
and with it the residual, whatever the target. There both $1/\sigma_i$ and $\lvert r_i\rvert$ are
largest, and the gain per unit of budget grows like $1/\sigma_i^2$. The gradient of a targeted attack
therefore points mostly into the directions in which the pseudoinverse keeps amplified noise, which
is part of no target, and cancelling it brings the range component closer to the ground truth as
well. The attacked initial reconstruction of the first test sample is accordingly closer to the
ground truth than the clean one (Figure~\ref{fig:example-nsn-zero}). A targeted attack on the
Nullspace Network thus denoises its range component as a side effect, while it damages the
null-space component.\\
The other runs confirm this reading, since the effect depends on how far below the noise level the
truncation reaches. By the heuristic of Section~\ref{sec:pinv}, a retained direction is dominated by
noise if its relative singular value lies below $\sigma_\text{rel}$. Where $\tau$ lies far below the
noise level, at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.02$ and $0.05$ and at $\tau=4.4\cdot10^{-4}$
(Section~\ref{sec:results-tau}), the attack towards the zero image lowers the range error of the
Nullspace Network on practically every sample; at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.05$ the attacked reconstruction
is even closer to the ground truth than the clean one on $98\%$ of the samples. As $\tau$ approaches
the noise level the effect fades, and where $\tau$ exceeds it, at $\tau=1.1\cdot10^{-2}$ and
$6.2\cdot10^{-2}$, it reverses: there the attack raises the range error on most samples.\\
Figure~\ref{fig:example-resnet-target} shows the first test sample under the attack towards another
sample for the residual network. The relative error rises more than tenfold, but the SSIM stays near
$0.9$: the attacked reconstruction shows a bright, sharply bounded sliver attached to the left
of the ellipse. This is structure of the kind a real object could
have, produced by a perturbation no larger than the noise. For the Nullspace Network
(Figure~\ref{fig:example-nsn-zero}) the attack towards the zero image leaves the relative error
practically unchanged: the fine noise texture of the clean reconstruction is largely gone, and
faint streaks run through the image instead.\\

A limitation of the current evaluation should be stated. The metrics of Section~\ref{sec:metrics}
measure the distance of the attacked reconstruction to the ground truth, not to the target. They
show how much a targeted attack damages the reconstruction, but not how close it comes to its
target, which is the actual measure of its success.

\subsection{Epoch study}\label{sec:results-epoch}
Figure~\ref{fig:epoch} shows the result of the epoch study at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$, where the fixed
budget $\varepsilon=0.01$ of the study coincides with the noise level and the models are attacked with the untargeted attack.
Neither network overfits. For both, the validation loss follows the training loss throughout the $50$ epochs,
and the lowest validation loss is reached in one of the last two epochs. This means overfitting does
not play a role in the attackability of our models.\\
For the residual network the median adversarial error does not grow with training. After a rise during
the first epochs to a peak at epoch $8$, it falls, with large fluctuations from one epoch to the next,
to less than half of that peak, while the clean error falls by almost an order of magnitude. In
absolute terms the network thus becomes more robust as it learns. Less so, if it is compared to its
clean error: the ratio of adversarial to clean error grows about fivefold over training. The
fluctuations between neighbouring epochs are large compared with the smooth loss curves.
%PersonalTODO: Maybe adjust scale of y axis to be consistent
This suggests that the attackability depends considerably on where exactly training stopped.\\
For the Nullspace Network the median adversarial error is flat from the first epoch on, while
the clean error falls only slightly.
The epoch study attacks the total error, and against the Nullspace Network this
attack grows the range component, which does not depend on the weights at all. The flat curve is
therefore a statement about the pseudoinverse, not about training.
An epoch study of the Nullspace Network that is informative about the network has to use the
null-space attack, and at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$ the study was repeated with it
(Figure~\ref{fig:epoch-null}). Measured on the null-space component, the Nullspace Network behaves
like the residual network. Its clean null-space error falls twentyfold over training. Its attacked
null-space error rises over the first epochs to a peak at epoch $3$ and then falls, with large
fluctuations, to less than half of that peak. The residual network, attacked in the same way,
follows the same course, with its peak at epoch $8$. For both networks the ratio of attacked to clean
null-space error grows more than tenfold over training. Both learned corrections thus become more
robust in absolute terms as they learn and less so relative to their clean error, and at the end of
training their attacked null-space errors are close to each other, as in the attack suite of
Section~\ref{sec:results-general}.\\
The epoch studies at the other three noise levels show the same two patterns. Since the budget is
fixed at $\varepsilon=0.01$ there, only the shapes of the curves can be compared, not their heights
(Section~\ref{sec:epoch}). The adversarial error of the residual network peaks within the first six
epochs and then falls, while its ratio to the clean error grows; that of the Nullspace Network stays
flat throughout. Neither network overfits at any noise level: the lowest validation loss is reached
at epoch $46$ or later in all eight trainings, and in the ten trainings of the further runs of
Sections~\ref{sec:results-tau} and~\ref{sec:results-data} as well.

The null-restricted gain of the Nullspace Network is about $40\%$ lower than that of the residual

attacker can make.\\

\subsection{The choice of the truncation}\label{sec:results-tau}
Figure~\ref{fig:tau} shows the truncation study of Section~\ref{sec:pinv}. At the reference threshold
$\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$ its curves reproduce the pseudoinverse errors of Table~\ref{tab:clean} to within a
few percent. The study evaluates phantoms from the training set with the
closed-form expression of Section~\ref{sec:pinv}, whereas the table was measured on the test set with
the operators of the pipeline, so this agreement is also a check of the study itself.\\
The threshold at which the pseudoinverse error is smallest grows with the noise, from
$\tau=6.1\cdot10^{-3}$ at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.005$ to $\tau=8.7\cdot10^{-2}$ at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.05$,
and lies at $\tau=1.1\cdot10^{-2}$ for $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$.
The reference threshold is thus below the optimum of the pseudoinverse at every noise level, and
increasingly so as the noise grows: it keeps more directions than the pseudoinverse can use, and at
$\sigma_\text{rel}=0.05$ it triples the pseudoinverse error. The
Nullspace Network inherits this range error unchanged, so its total errors in Table~\ref{tab:clean}
are larger than they would be at a pseudoinverse-optimal threshold. The residual network, which
removes more than $90\%$ of the range component, can be expected to be much less affected. The
threshold that is optimal for the pseudoinverse need not be optimal for a network that corrects it,
since a larger threshold also enlarges the null space the network has to fill.
%PersonalTODO: Add reference to optimal tau study "big in japan"
At $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$ the optimal threshold enlarges $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ by a quarter.\\
The per-channel results above are stated on the split at $\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$, and whether they depend
on it can only be settled by runs at different thresholds. The study proposes three
candidates at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$: the pseudoinverse optimum $\tau=1.1\cdot10^{-2}$, and the
thresholds $\tau=4.4\cdot10^{-4}$ and $\tau=6.2\cdot10^{-2}$, which halve and double the dimension of
the null space. All three were run at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$, each through the
whole pipeline from data generation to the attacks, since $\tau$ is part of the data.
Table~\ref{tab:clean-tau} gives their clean errors next to those at the reference threshold.

The pseudoinverse behaves as the study predicts. As $\tau$ grows, its range error falls by a factor of
almost forty and its null-space error doubles; its total error is smallest at the optimum
$\tau=1.1\cdot10^{-2}$. At $\tau=4.4\cdot10^{-4}$ it amplifies by up to $1/\tau\approx2\,300$, and its
reconstruction is dominated by amplified noise, with an error twice the norm of the phantom.\\
The Nullspace Network inherits the range floor exactly at every threshold, so its total error follows
the range error of the pseudoinverse, not its total error: it falls with every step in $\tau$. Of the
four thresholds its best is the largest, well above the optimum of the pseudoinverse. At
$\tau=6.2\cdot10^{-2}$ the pseudoinverse is worse than at its optimum, but the Nullspace Network
more than halves its error once more, because the larger threshold moves error from the range
component, which the Nullspace Network inherits, into the null space, which it has learned to fill.
The residual network is best at the optimum of the pseudoinverse. At $\tau=4.4\cdot10^{-4}$ its
reconstruction degrades markedly, since it has to remove a range error twice the norm of the phantom
from its input. The gap between the two architectures narrows as $\tau$ grows, from a factor of $12$
at the reference threshold to $2.6$ at $\tau=6.2\cdot10^{-2}$. On the null-space component the order of
Table~\ref{tab:clean} persists at every threshold: the Nullspace Network has the lower clean
null-space error.\\
Under attack, the threshold sets the amplification of the pseudoinverse. The bound
$\varepsilon(1+\sigma_\text{rel})/\tau$ of Section~\ref{sec:results-general} on the growth of the range
component scales with $1/\tau$, and at the three smaller thresholds the median growth under the
untargeted attack follows it, at $36$ to $38\%$ of the bound each time (Table~\ref{tab:prop}). The range
damage the untargeted attack does to the Nullspace Network thus scales with $1/\tau$. At
$\tau=6.2\cdot10^{-2}$ it falls short of this, to $28\%$ of a bound that has itself become small, and
the untargeted attack turns elsewhere: most of the attacked error of the Nullspace Network now lies in
the null space. As on the multi-ellipse phantoms at $\tau=2.5\cdot10^{-2}$
(Section~\ref{sec:results-data}), the untargeted attack on the Nullspace Network becomes a null-space
attack by itself once $\tau$ is large enough. This is also the only run of this work in which the
untargeted attack leaves the Nullspace Network with the smaller total error; under the null-space
attack the two are level.\\
The comparison on the null space does depend on the threshold (Tables~\ref{tab:robustness}
and~\ref{tab:gains}). Relative to the growth of the residual network, the null-space error of the
Nullspace Network grows a third less at the smallest threshold, about a tenth less at the two middle
ones, and $6\%$ more at the largest, where the difference lies just outside sampling error. The
advantage of the Nullspace Network thus shrinks as the null space grows and changes sign at twice
the reference dimension; at the two middle thresholds the differences cannot be told apart.\\
Only at the smallest threshold is the difference large. There the null-space error of the Nullspace
Network grows less on $95\%$ of the samples. This advantage appears at a threshold at
which the Nullspace Network fails without any attack: its clean error is twice the norm of the
phantom. The null-space attack moves both networks far from their clean input, more than tripling
the error of the initial reconstruction. It drives the null-space errors of both
networks beyond the norm of the phantom itself, while the range error of the Nullspace Network, which
the attacker cannot help growing, reaches almost seven times that norm.\\
Over the thresholds from $\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$ to $\tau=6.2\cdot10^{-2}$, which include the pseudoinverse
optimum and double the null space, the comparison keeps the character it has at the reference
threshold: the null-space attack raises the median null-space errors of both networks by a factor of
$20$ or more, and the attacked medians of the two differ by less than $10\%$. Within this range
the negative result does not hang on the choice $\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$. The one clear advantage of the
Nullspace Network in the null space appears only at a threshold at which it fails without any attack.

\subsection{The phantoms}\label{sec:results-data}

motivates this simplification, but it also limits what the results can claim: a single ellipse
offers the attacker little structure to deform and the network little structure to protect. The
repetition on DIVAL's multi-ellipse phantoms at one noise level tests whether the central findings
carry over, namely the exact range floor of the Nullspace Network and the equal null-space vulnerability of the two networks,
which is an empirical finding.
The run is repeated at $\tau=2.5\cdot10^{-2}$, the optimum of the
pseudoinverse that the truncation study finds for these phantoms at $\sigma_\text{rel}=0.01$.\\
