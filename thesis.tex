\section{Introduction}
Computed Tomography is an important diagnosis tool in modern medicine, it can be used to identify various injuries or diseases inside the body. Examples include screening the chest for viral and bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis, lung cancer, the bones and teeth for fractures and infections and many more\parencite{CT-Scan}. Reconstructing the image of the tissue from the measurements poses an inverse problem that is usually solved using the filtered back projection. This reconstruction, however, produces artifacts and unsatisfactory image quality \parencite{CT-LA-CNN}, when applied to measurements resulting from a Limited Angle CT-Scan. Due to the severe Ill-posedness of the Limited Angle CT-Scan, an accurate reconstruction method for the Limited Angle setting is yet to be found. Reconstructions using neural networks form a promising approach, as neural networks have been used successfully in different kinds of inverse problems.
One big disadvantage of such reconstruction methods, however, is their vulnerability towards deliberate manipulations of the input \parencite{instab}. Since CT-Scans are used all over the world, such vulnerable reconstruction methods would pose a risk in patient safety. That is, why this thesis investigates the robustness of the reconstruction method.\\
Before we get into the details of how the reconstruction method works, and how we plan to attack it, we first need to understand, how computed Tomography and Limited Angle Computed Tomography in particular actually work.
\subsection{Limited-angle CT}
To understand how a CT-scan works, let us first look at general X-ray imaging. Sending an x-ray through a body tissue and measuring its intensity at the other side of the body is a way of measuring the average density of the tissue the ray was sent through. By arranging x-ray emitters and detectors in a plane and putting the tissue one wants to investigate in between them, an x-ray image can be produced.\\
CT uses the same mechanism of tissue absorbing x-rays based on the tissues density. There are two main ways of doing CT: fan-beam and parallel-beam. Since fan-beam is the more complex one, in this work we restrict ouselfs to parallel-beam CT and whenever CT will be mentioned in this work, we are talking about parallel-beam CT. In CT one uses emitters and detectors lying in the slice plane perpendicular to the rotation axis. Rotating this array of x-ray sources and detectors around the tissue generates different measurements for each measured angle; Figure \ref{fig:parallel-beam} shows the resulting parallel-beam geometry. Usually one rotates the full $180^\circ$ to not miss any features. These measurements can be composed into one 2D Image called a sinogram. Each row of a sinogram corresponds to one angle, each column to one detector. Such a sinogram can then be used to reconstruct the axial image or slice.

\\
The number of measurements required during a CT-scan depends on the number of angles that one measures and the amount of detectors used during the scan. More measurements mean longer measuring process and also increased exposure of the tissue to radiation.\\
Consequently research on ways to reduce the amount of measurements, while still producing sufficiently accurate reconstructions is of both economic and medicinal interest. One way to reduce the amount of measurements is to reduce the angles one is measuring at. This can be done by simply measuring fewer angles in total while retaining the total coverage of angles, i.e. measuring in $2^\circ$ instead of $1^\circ$ steps, reducing the total amount of measurements by one half.\\
Another way, which will also be the focus of this work, relies on the reduction of the total angles covered. Instead of considering measurements across the full range from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$ one only measures from $0^\circ$ to $120^\circ$. This is called limited-angle CT. It is severely ill-posed and methods like the filtered back projection, which work well for conventional CT-scans, fail to provide good reconstruction images \parencite{CT-LA-CNN}.\\
The reconstruction method used in this thesis is not only based on neural networks, but also on a classical reconstruction step. Classical reconstruction methods better understood and provide a good initial construction, which serves as an useful input to the neural network. The network therefore only needs to learns the negative error of the classical reconstruction method to the ground truth. 

\subsection{Pseudoinverse}\label{sec:pseudoinverse}
The Radon transform is the function, that can be used to model the x-ray measurement process. It builds the line integral of a function and is defined in the following way.
$$\mathcal{R}f(L):=\int_Lf(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}$$
Where $R$ denotes the radon transform, $f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ is an arbitrary, measurable function and $L\subset\Omega$ the line along which the measurement is taken.
Since in Computed Tomography the lines are strictly defined by an offset $s$ and an angle $\theta$, we can also write this in the following way:
$$\mathcal{R}f(\theta,s):=\int_\mathbb{R}f(s\omega_\theta + t\omega_\theta^\perp)dt, \qquad \omega_\theta=(\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})$$
It is easy to see, that this is a linear operator. Under the assumption, that the $\Omega$ is expressible as a finite pixel-basis and that we only make $m\in\mathbb{N}$ measurements $(\theta_i, s_i) \quad i\in\{1,...,m\}$, we can use a Matrix to model the x-ray measurement process. Assigning each pixel a number from $1$ to $n$ allows us to define our function $f(x)=\sum_jc_j\varphi_j(x)=\sum_jc_j\mathds{1}_j(x) \quad x\in\{1,...,n\} $ as a finite sum of weighted basis functions. These weights can be grouped into one vector $c \in\mathbb{R}^n$. Now applying the Radon transform and using linearity gives us for the measurement $y_i$ corresponding to $(\theta_i,s_i)$ the following equation.$$y_i = \mathcal{R}f(\theta_i,s_i) = \sum_jc_j\mathcal{R}\varphi_j(\theta_i,s_i)=\sum_jc_j\mathbf{A}_{ij}=e_i^T\mathbf{A}c$$
Aggregating every measurement into one row we receive the linear equation with $\mathbf{A}$ defined by $\mathbf{A}_{ij}:=\mathcal{R}\varphi_j(\theta_i,s_i)$. $$y=\mathbf{A}c$$
Since the parameters of the radon transform $\theta$ and $s$ along with the choice of basis functions uniquely define $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ we write it as $\mathbf{A}_\mathcal{R}$.\\
This simplifies the implementation and allows us to use the pseudoinverse $A_\mathcal{R}^+$ to get a reconstruction of the original image. Due to a large requirement of memory and computational power, this approach is not being used in clinical CT. Images in clinical CT require $512 \times 512$ pixels and low-dose clinical CT works with $700-900$ detector elements and about $1000$ projections per $360^\circ$ slice, which corresponds to $500$ projections per $180^\circ$. In this way we obtain a Matrix of size $ 350\,000\times262\,144$ \parencite{Flohr2013}. Singular Value Decomposition for matrices in this size, take a long time and are therefore not feasible in clinical CT. In this laboratory setting however, our matrix is smaller and using it, simplifies concepts like null space and its complement. This will also be of importance, when we talk about null space networks and error decomposition in null space and null space complement.\\
The construction of the Pseudoinverse is done using the singular value decomposition. It is a useful tool that generalizes the eigenvalue decomposition to non square matrices. A general Matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ can be written in the following way:
$$\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^*$$
Where $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ and $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ are unitary matrices and $\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ is a diagonal matrix with $r$ non negative real numbers $\sigma_i$ on the diagonal, where $r$ is the rank of the matrix $\mathbf{A}$.
We fix the ordering once and use it for the rest of this work. The singular values are sorted in descending order,
$$\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\dots\geq\sigma_r>0,$$
and the columns of $\mathbf{U}$ and of $\mathbf{V}$ are ordered to match, so
that the $i$-th column of each belongs to $\sigma_i$. Two things depend on this
convention. $\sigma_1=\sigma_{\max}=\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_2$ is the operator
norm, which is what the truncation threshold of the next paragraph is measured
against; and ``the first $k$ columns of $\mathbf{V}$'' is then an unambiguous
description of the vectors a truncation keeps. The decomposition returned by
$\texttt{torch.linalg.svd}$ is already sorted this way. The Pseudoinverse of $\mathbf{A}$ is then easily constructed. $$\mathbf{A}^+=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{+}\mathbf{U}^*=\mathbf{V}
\begin{pNiceArray}{ccc|c}[margin, cell-space-limits=2pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sigma_1} &        &                     & \Block{3-1}{\text{\Huge 0}} \\
                    & \Ddots &                     &                             \\
                    &        & \dfrac{1}{\sigma_r} &                             \\
\hline
\Block{1-3}{\text{\Huge 0}} & & & \text{\Huge 0}                                  \\
\end{pNiceArray}\mathbf{U}^*$$
This pseudoinverse can then be used to convert a sinogram back to the axial image. Since in real world application sinogram data always comes with noise, To better represent real world applications we are also going to need to add some noise to the data, as real world measurements can never be noise free. But Noisy data reveals an issue with the pseudoinverse. The severe ill-posedness means, singular values $\sigma_i$ decay exponentially to zero. For the Pseudoinverse, this means its singular values $1/\sigma_i$ grow exponentially. Since noise is spread evenly across the singular values of the pseudoinverse, it is amplified exponentially and can overshadow the actual signal strength. To counteract this, one can use a truncated pseudoinverse, setting all singular values $\sigma_i$, smaller than some threshold $\tau$, to zero. When inverting, they remain zero and the amplification can be bounded with $1/\tau$.\\
This gives rise to a nontrivial null space $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ i.e. non trivial structures, that when measured give $0$ measurements. This allows us to decompose the space $\mathbb{R}^n$ into $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})^\perp$ which will be of use later in this work.\\
This is the point to fix the notation for the three operators that appear
throughout, because they are easy to confuse. $\mathbf{A}_\text{full}$ is the
system matrix of \emph{all} projection angles and $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ its
restriction to the measured ones; $\mathbf{U}_k,\Sigma_k,\mathbf{V}_k$ are the
factors of $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ that survive the truncation at $\tau$, and
$\mathbf{A}_{la}^{+}=\mathbf{V}_k\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$ is the
truncated pseudoinverse built from them. In the theory of this and the next
chapter only the limited-angle operator ever occurs, so we abbreviate
$\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{A}_{la}$ and write $\mathbf{A}^{+}$ for its truncated
pseudoinverse. Chapter \ref{sec:implementation}, where the distinction between
the full and the limited-angle matrix does matter, uses the subscripted names
throughout.\vspace{10pt}\\

From now on, the space $\mathbf{Y}$ denotes the space of sinograms or more generally measurements and the space $\mathbf{X}$ the space of all images. Both spaces are finite dimensional.
\subsection{Residual UNet}\label{sec:resunet}
Before we get into what makes the residual network special, we need to understand how a general neural network is built. A neural network consists of connected neurons, which are usually grouped into layers. Each neuron has an input with weights, attached to each input, an offset, an activation function and an output. The activation function is usually nonlinear. One neuron can therefore be modeled in the following way:
$$\mathbf{y} = \phi(\sum_{i=1}^nw_i\mathbf{x}_i + b)\quad w_i,\mathbf{x}_i,b\in\mathbb{R}$$
Where $\mathbf{y}$ is the output, $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the activation function, $w_i\mathbf{x}_i$ are the weighted inputs and $b$ is the offset. The layers of the neural network are executed in sequence, the input layer receives the network input and feeds its output into the next layer. Iteratively each hidden layer is passed through until the data arrives at the output layer and produces the network output.\\ 
Up to some threshold, deeper models perform better than shallow ones \parencite{deeper-better}. Nevertheless, it has been reported, that adding more layers to a suitably deep model, increases its training error \parencite{res}. This is surprising, as it is easy to find a trivial deep mode, performing on par with the shallow model. It can be constructed by supplementing the shallow model with layers that are the identity. Such a model possesses a larger depth, but has equivalent performance as the shallow, used to construct it. This indicates, that some deep networks are harder to optimize, than shallow ones. 
Since residual networks have been introduced in 2015, they have become a popular Convolutional Neural Network architecture. They address this degradation problem by learning the residual instead of the whole mapping. Let $\mathcal{H}:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}$ be the true mapping from measurements to images and $\mathcal{I}:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}$ be the identity, if measurements and images live in the same space and otherwise an easy to calculate classical reconstruction map. The residual mapping learned by the neural network is then given by: $\mathcal{G}:= \mathcal{H} - \mathcal{I}$ \parencite{res-org}.\\
In our setting the two spaces differ, so $\mathcal{I}$, the classical reconstruction
map, is the truncated pseudoinverse $\mathbf{A}^+$, and the residual is realised
by a network $N_\theta:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}$ acting on the initial reconstruction. Writing
$\mathbf{x}_\text{init}:=\mathbf{A}^+\mathbf{y}$, the full reconstruction map is
$$\mathcal{F}_\theta:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X},\qquad
  \mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}_\text{init} + N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init}).$$
We refer to $N_\theta$ as the network and to $\mathcal{F}_\theta$ as the reconstruction
method; both symbols are used with these meanings for the remainder of this work.\\
So far we have only talked about the general architecture of this model. Now we also need to address the architecture of the neural network itself. For this we are going to use the UNet. It is a special type of convolutional neural network, that maps images to images. The UNet can be segmented into two paths, one contracting and one expansive path. The contracting path is built on the repeated application of convolutions, each followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) and a max pooling operation for down sampling\parencite{U-NET}.\\
A convolution restricts the number and location of the neurons in the next layer, that a neuron can influence, a convolution with a kernel of size $3\times3$ can only influence the neuron corresponding to itself and its surrounding $8$ neighbors. The ReLU function is the identity for positive input values and maps everything else to $0$. The max pooling operation splits the output of a layer into different pools and combines them into one output by taking the largest value in the pool. If for instance you have a $4\times4$ layer, max pooling outputs values in a $3\times 3$ or $2\times 2$ grid depending on the application.
\[
\begin{pNiceMatrix}[margin]
\Block[draw=red,fill=red!15]{2-2}{}
\sigma_1    & \sigma_2    & \sigma_3 \Block[draw=red,fill=red!30]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_4\\
\sigma_5    & \sigma_6    & \sigma_7    & \sigma_8\\
\sigma_9 \Block[draw=red]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_{10} & \sigma_{11}\Block[draw=red]{2-2}{}
 & \sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{13} & \sigma_{14} & \sigma_{15} & \sigma_{16}
\end{pNiceMatrix}
\begin{pNiceMatrix}[margin]
\Block[draw=blue,fill=blue!15]{2-2}{}
\sigma_1    & \sigma_2   \Block[draw=blue,fill=blue!30]{2-2}{} & \sigma_3 \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_4\\
\sigma_5  \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}  & \sigma_6  \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}  & \sigma_7 \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}   & \sigma_8\\
\sigma_9 \Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}
   & \sigma_{10}\Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{} & \sigma_{11}\Block[draw=blue]{2-2}{}

\end{pNiceMatrix}

This figure illustrates the different applications, the red highlighted matrix outputs a $2\times 2$ grid of values, since it moves two values in between steps and therefore does not take any values twice. The blue one outputs a $3\times 3$ grid. It only moves one value in between steps and therefore takes some values multiple times. The U-Net uses the approach in the red matrix, which leads to a reduction in the number of neurons and weights. The memory gained by reducing neurons and weights is then used to introduce new feature channels. The $2\times2$ max pooling halves the spatial resolution but leaves the number of channels unchanged. The number of feature channels is then doubled by the first convolution of the following block, which uses twice as many filters as that block has input channels. After the contractive path, the expansive path applies a form of upsampling with another $2\times 2$ convolution, halving the number of feature channels. The upsampled data is then combined with some data from the corresponding downsampling step and twice more convolved, followed by a ReLU application. The original U-Net Network has 23 convolutional layers\parencite{U-NET}. Its original application has been for segmentation purposes, however it is also applicable for image to image prediction.
\subsection{Null space Network}\label{sec:nsn}
The Residual U-Net is a so called unconstrained Network, it can modify the data, however it desires, to predict its output. This can lead to problems, since it can happen, that the prediction made by the Residual U-Net does not necessarily agree with the measurements it was fed. One possible remedy to this issue is the so called Null Space Network. It's architecture is identical to the Residual U-Net with the exception, that the output of the Null Space Network is Projected onto the Null Space of the Forward Operator (i.e. the measurement process). This guarantees data consistency and we had hoped, that consequently the resulting network would be more robust towards adversarial attacks. Adversarial attacks on both residual UNet and Null space networks however, have shown, that this is not the case and that resistance towards adversarial attacks or robustness, do not come for free with data consistency.\\
To use a Null Space Network, one has to know a Projection onto the Null Space of the Forward Operator or Measurement Process. This is where the Singular Value Decomposition comes in useful once more, the unitary matrix $\mathbf{V}$ can be used to calculate the Nullspace projection in the following way.
With the descending ordering of Section \ref{sec:pseudoinverse}, the $k$
directions that are kept are the \emph{first} $k$ columns of $\mathbf{V}$, and
collecting them in the matrix $\mathbf{V}_k$ allows us to calculate the Projection $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ by
$$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})} = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^*$$
This allows us to express the Nullspace Network as a concatenation of the  projection $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ and the network, where $\mathbf{x}_\text{init}$ is an initial reconstruction obtained by some classical reconstruction method, in our case the Pseudoinverse $\mathbf{A}^+$.$$\mathbf{x}_\text{out} = \mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}_\text{init} + \mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$$
By Applying $\mathbf{A}$ to the above equation, it is easy to see, that the Network output can not modify the measurements gained from the initial reconstruction. The Network is provably data consistent. This also means, that it can never change the measurements. Any errors, that live in the input it has been given and produce non zero measurements, can never be corrected by the network. $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})} = 0 \implies \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\text{out} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_\text{init}$$\\
This is particularly an issue, considering that some noise will always be visible in the measurements, even after the application of the pseudoinverse. The error generated by this noise is not correctable by the Nullspace Network. Decomposing the reconstruction error $\mathbf{e}:=\mathbf{x}_\text{out}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger$ into its nullspace and nullspace-complement components makes this precise. The nullspace-complement component $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})\mathbf{e}$ of the reconstruction error is identical to that of the initial reconstruction, whatever the network predicts. We will refer to this as the \emph{range floor}. In the nullspace-complement the Nullspace Network can therefore never produce results that are comparable to those of the unconstrained residual network, which is free to correct this component and does.\\
The projection also gives us a decomposition of the space of reconstructions into the null space $\mathcal{N}$ and its complement $\mathcal{N}^\perp$. This will be used not only to analyze our attacks later, but also to construct a new type of attack, used in this work. 
\subsection{Adversarial Attacks}
An adversarial attack is the targeted construction of a small perturbation to a models input, that maximizes the error of the perturbation. Depending on how this error is measured and how much the threat model knows about the model they can differ significantly \parencite{advers}.
In this work we want to use adversarial attacks to investigate the robustness of the residual UNet and the Null space network. The next section describes adversarial attacks in more detail and explains modifications we made to the general attack framework to better evaluate our models.\\
\section{Theory behind Adversarial Attacks}
Let $\mathcal{F}_\theta:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{X}$ denote a reconstruction method, that is, an
initial reconstruction followed by a trained network, going from the space of measurements to
the space of reconstructions. Everything in this section applies to both of the architectures
of the previous chapter, the unconstrained residual network and the Nullspace Network, which
differ only in whether the projection $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ is applied to the
output of $N_\theta$. Where the distinction matters we say so explicitly.
\subsection{Adversarial Attacks}\label{sec:attacks}
An adversarial attack is heavily dependent on the threat model. It defines how much information the attack is given and how we measure its success. The attack type that we are going to investigate in this work is the evasion type attack. As mentioned before, the aim is to find a small perturbation $\delta^* \in\mathbf{Y}$ defined by:
$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^*\;=\argmax_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_p \leq \varepsilon}\;\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta}), \mathbf{x}^\dagger )$$
where $\mathbf{y}$ are the measurements, $\mathbf{x}^\dagger$ is the ground truth corresponding to them, $\mathcal{L}$ is a loss function and $\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is the budget of the attack.
Adversarial attacks can use different strategies to find such a perturbation. In this work we relied on the projected gradient descent optimizer (PGD), Madry et al. \parencite{PGD} claim this to be the strongest attack utilizing the local first order information of the network. Suppose we want to attack the groundtruth, measurement pair $(\mathbf{x}^\dagger, \mathbf{y})$. 
Projected gradient descent is a general method for minimising a differentiable function over
a closed convex set: one takes a gradient step and projects the result back onto the set.
Here the set is the ball of radius $\varepsilon$ around the clean sinogram,
$$\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon:=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{Y}\;:\;\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2\leq\varepsilon\},$$
so that every iterate stays within the budget of the true measurement. Starting from a
random point $\mathbf{y}_0\in\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon$, each step is calculated as follows:
$$\mathbf{y}_{n+1}:=\Pi_{\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon}\left( \mathbf{y}_n-\alpha\mathbf{g}_n\right), \qquad \mathbf{g}_n:=\nabla_\mathbf{z}\, \mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{z}),\,\mathbf{x}^\dagger\right)   \Big|_{\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}_n}$$
where $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ is the step size, $\Pi_{\mathbf{y}+B_\varepsilon}$ is the
projection onto that ball and $\mathcal{L}$ is the same loss function as above. Note that
the ball is centred at $\mathbf{y}$ and not at the origin: we are looking for a small
perturbation of one specific sinogram, not for a sinogram of small norm.\\
Two changes turn this minimiser into the attack we use. First, we maximise instead of
minimise, so the gradient step is added rather than subtracted. Second, the raw gradient is
replaced by the direction of steepest ascent with respect to the norm in which the budget is
measured. For a step of length $\alpha$ that direction maximises
$\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{d}\rangle$ over $\lVert\mathbf{d}\rVert\leq\alpha$, which for the
two norms of interest gives
$$\ell_\infty:\quad \alpha\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{g}),\qquad\qquad
  \ell_2:\quad \alpha\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert_2}.$$
Madry et al. \parencite{PGD} state the algorithm with the sign function, because their threat
model is bounded in the $\ell_\infty$ norm. Our budget is measured in the $\ell_2$ norm, so we
use the normalised gradient instead, and the step rule and the constraint set are then
expressed in the same norm.\\
A last adaption concerns the feasible set. We investigate only those perturbations of the
measurements that an attacker could realistically produce, that is, perturbations which are
themselves measurements taken at the angles the scanner actually covers. We therefore
additionally require the perturbation to lie in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})$, which
gives the feasible set $S:=\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A})\cap B_\varepsilon$. Writing the
iteration in terms of the perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}_n:=\mathbf{y}_n-\mathbf{y}$
instead of the sinogram itself, which translates both the iterate and the constraint set and
therefore describes the same algorithm, the update rule becomes
$$\delta_{n+1}:=\Pi_{S}\left( \delta_n+\alpha\frac{\mathbf{g}_n}{\lVert\mathbf{g}_n\rVert_2}\right),\qquad \mathbf{g}_n:=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta}),\,\mathbf{x}^\dagger\right)\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\delta}=\delta_n}$$
with the starting point $\delta_0$ drawn at random from $S$.
This is the blueprint for every attack we used to test the robustness of our models. The attacks differ from one another only in the choice of the loss $\mathcal{L}$, which the following two sections describe.\\
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks on Subspaces}\label{sec:subspace}
With the blueprint we have just given, it is already possible to perform successful attacks
on both models. During our work, however, we noticed that when attacking a Nullspace
Network with the plain squared-error loss, the error found by the attack grew almost
entirely in the nullspace complement. By the range floor of Section \ref{sec:nsn} this is
precisely the component the network does not control: it is inherited unchanged from the
initial reconstruction, so an attack that grows it is exploiting the architecture rather
than the learned reconstruction. The resulting error is large, but it is not evidence of a
vulnerability, and it is not comparable to what the same attack achieves against the
unconstrained residual network, which is free to correct that component.\\
The remedy is not a further restriction of the perturbation, but a change of the
objective. Let
$$\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{\delta}):=\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathbf{x}^\dagger$$
denote the reconstruction error. Instead of its total energy we maximise the energy of a
single component of it,
$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}}:=\lVert \mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\rVert_2^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}:=\lVert (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})})\,\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\rVert_2^2 .$$
Both projections are linear and differentiable, so the attack of the previous section
applies unchanged; only the loss whose gradient is taken is replaced. Maximising
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}}$ forces the attack to damage the nullspace component, which is
the only part of the reconstruction subject to the influence of the Nullspace Network. Since the residual network is unconstrained, we can compare the robustness of the two different networks better by constraining the attack to the nullspace component.
Maximising $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$ gives the complementary attack and serves as a control. For the Nullspace Network, it can only ever attack the Pseudoinverse. For the Residual UNet, it shows how far the measurements of the reconstruction can be moved by a small perturbation.
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks using Examples}\label{sec:targeted}
Another modification to our attack is made by altering the attack objective so that it no longer drives the reconstruction \emph{away} from the ground truth, but \emph{towards} a prescribed image. Attacks of this kind are called targeted.
Let $\mathbf{t}\in\mathbf{X}$ be a target image and replace the loss by
$$\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{t}:=-\lVert\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathbf{t}\rVert_2^2 .$$
Since the attack maximises $\mathcal{L}$, maximising $\mathcal{L}_\mathbf{t}$ minimises the distance between the reconstruction and the target, and the update rule of the previous section can again be used unchanged.\\
We use two choices of target. The first is the zero image, $\mathbf{t}=0$, which asks how
far towards an empty reconstruction an attacker can push the model within the budget. The
second is the ground truth of a \emph{different} sample, so that the attack attempts to
make the reconstruction of one object resemble another. It asks not whether the reconstruction can be corrupted, but
whether it can be corrupted into something that still looks like a plausible scan of a
different patient. A targeted attack is a strictly harder problem than an untargeted one,
because the attacker must reach one particular point of image space rather than any point
far from the ground truth, and we should expect correspondingly smaller errors at the same
budget.
\section{Numerical implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
This chapter describes how the operators, networks and attacks of the previous
two chapters are realised in code. The implementation is written in Python and
rests on two libraries: PyTorch \parencite{Pytorch} provides the tensor algebra,
the neural network layers and, most importantly for this work, reverse-mode
automatic differentiation, and the ASTRA Toolbox \parencite{Astra} provides the
tomographic geometry. Every number reported in this thesis was produced with PyTorch 2.3.1 on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000.\\
The central object of the implementation is the \emph{tensor}: a
multidimensional array that carries a shape, a numeric type and the device it
lives on (CPU or GPU), and that optionally records the operations which produced
it. That record is the computational graph, and it is what lets PyTorch
differentiate a value with respect to any tensor it was computed from. Two
shapes occur throughout. An image is a tensor of shape $(B,C,H,W)$ --- batch,
channel, height, width --- and a sinogram is a tensor of shape
$(B,C,n_\theta,n_d)$ --- batch, channel, projection angle, detector element.
Here $B$ is the number of samples processed simultaneously and $C=1$, since our
images are greyscale. Every operator described below maps between these two
shapes and acts on a whole batch in a single call.\\
One convention deserves to be stated in advance, because it recurs in every
later section. The limited-angle operators do not return a shortened sinogram
containing only the measured rows; they return a \emph{full-shape} sinogram in
which the rows belonging to unmeasured angles are set to zero. Every sinogram in
a run therefore has the same shape, whether it was produced by the full or by
the limited-angle operator, and the restriction to the measured angles is itself
an operator, namely the multiplication with a $0/1$ mask, we denote this mask in the following with $\mathbf{M}$.
$\mathbf{M}$ is a tensor of shape $(n_\theta,n_d)$ with $1$ in the rows corresponding to measured angles and $0$ elsewhere.
It is important to note, that $\mathbf{M}$ is different from the projection onto the space of viable measurements, $\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}$, since not every sinogram that is zero at the unmeasured angles can be obtained by measuring some image. So the space of viable measurements, $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$, is a proper subspace of the space of sinograms supported on the measured angles, i.e.\ of $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M})$. Both projections occur in the implementation and they are not interchangeable: it is $\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}$ that restricts the simulated noise of Section \ref{sec:data} and the adversarial perturbation of Section \ref{sec:adversarial}.\\
\subsection{Radon operator}\label{sec:radon}
For the implementation of the Radon operator, we used the Astra-Toolbox \parencite{Astra}. It is maintained by the University of Antwerp and supports different kinds of Radon operations. For this experimental setup, we used a 2D parallel-beam Computed Tomography. 
The first step to obtaining a radon operator in the Astra-Toolbox is done by defining a Volume geometry. It defines the size of the image we want to reconstruct, which in turn determines the size of the emitter and detector array.
The second descriptor is the projection geometry, which fixes the beam type
(\texttt{parallel}), the spacing of the detector elements, their number, and the
vector of projection angles. From the two descriptors a projector is built using
the \texttt{strip} model, which integrates over the strip that a detector
element actually sees rather than along an infinitely thin line. This is
precisely the discretisation assumed in Section \ref{sec:pseudoinverse}: a pixel basis on the image
side and a finite set of measurements $(\theta_i,s_i)$ on the sinogram side.\\
The geometry used for every experiment in this work is the following.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline
image resolution        & $128\times128$ pixels, so $n=16\,384$ \\
detector elements       & $n_d=182$ \\
projection angles       & $n_\theta=180$, equispaced over $[0^\circ,180^\circ)$ \\
limited-angle window    & $\varphi=[0^\circ,120^\circ)$, i.e.\ $120$ of the $180$ angles \\
pixel spacing           & $\mathrm{d}x=1$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The detector count is chosen as $\lfloor\sqrt2\cdot128\rfloor+1=182$, that is, at
least the length of the image diagonal, so that no corner of the image can fall
outside the detector array for any projection angle.\\
ASTRA is used to construct the system matrix itself rather than as a
matrix-free forward and backprojection pair, because the matrix is what gives
access to exact inversion: with its singular value decomposition at hand the
pseudoinverse and the null-space projection are closed-form linear operators
instead of the output of an iterative solver, and both are ordinary tensor
products, which is what lets an attack gradient travel through them. In Python
this is implemented in the following way:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
angles = np.linspace(0, 180, 180, endpoint=False)
vol_geom = astra.create_vol_geom(128, 128)
proj_geom = astra.create_proj_geom('parallel', 1.0, 182, angles)
projector_id = astra.create_projector('strip', proj_geom, vol_geom)
matrix_id = astra.projector.matrix(projector_id)
csr = astra.matrix.get(matrix_id)
\end{lstlisting}
This creates a projector, callable by its id, that can be used to calculate the radon transform of an image and in the final two lines, calculates a matrix, that can be used to perform the radon operation. 
The matrix is given as a \texttt{scipy.sparse.csr\_matrix}, compressed sparse row matrix, and will later be used to calculate the pseudoinverse and the nullspace projections.
In the following, let $\mathbf{A}_\text{full}:=\texttt{csr}$ be the sparse matrix with dimensions $182*180\times 128*128 = 32\,760\times16\,384$. 
From $\mathbf{A}_\text{full}$ we can construct the limited-angle matrix
$\mathbf{A}_{la}\in\mathbb{R}^{21\,840\times16\,384}$ obtained by keeping only the
rows belonging to the $120$ measured angles. ASTRA orders the rows angle-major, so row $i\cdot n_d+j$ holds detector
$j$ at angle $\theta_i$, $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ is obtained by only taking those rows corresponding to measured angles.
It is $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ that plays the
role of $\mathbf{A}$ everywhere in the theory.
$\mathbf{A}_{la}$ models the limited angle Computed Tomography measurement process the networks aim to invert and the attacks perturb.\\
Building the matrices and decomposing them is expensive but depends only on the
geometry, never on the data. Both are therefore cached on disk under a SHA-256
hash of the resolution, detector count, pixel spacing, angle vector,
limited-angle window and truncation threshold, so that a rerun with the same
geometry loads them instead of recomputing them.\\
\subsection{Pseudoinverse}\label{sec:pinv}
The pseudoinverse of Section \ref{sec:pseudoinverse} requires the singular value decomposition of
the system matrix, and this is the single most expensive computation in the
whole pipeline. $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ is densified in double precision and decomposed with
\texttt{torch.linalg.svd} on the GPU, where the dense copy occupies
$21\,840\cdot16\,384\cdot8\,\text{B}=2.9$\,GB. Without a GPU the same dense decomposition runs through LAPACK on the
CPU, which is the path the test suite takes.\\
%TODO: always keep matrix in single precision?
The decomposition itself is therefore always computed in double precision, and
that is also the precision the factors are written to the cache in. Single
precision enters only when the cached factors are \emph{loaded}: the adapter
casts them to its own dtype, which the data generator leaves at double and which
training and the attacks set to single. The paragraph on precision at the end of
this section returns to why.\\
The truncation described in Section \ref{sec:pseudoinverse} is applied relative to the largest
singular value. A direction is retained if
$$\sigma_i\;\geq\;\tau\,\sigma_{\max},\qquad \tau = 4\cdot10^{-3}.$$
Scaling the threshold by $\sigma_{\max}$ rather than fixing
it absolutely puts the cutoff in relation to the operator norm, so $\tau$ is
dimensionless and does not have to be re-tuned when the geometry --- and with that
the scale of $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ --- changes. It also controls the conditioning of
the retained operator directly, and it is worth spelling that out. A retained
direction satisfies $\sigma_i\geq\tau\sigma_{\max}$ by definition, so the
smallest retained singular value obeys $\sigma_{\min}\geq\tau\sigma_{\max}$ and
the condition number of the retained operator is
$$\frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\tau}\;=\;250 .$$
Equivalently $\lVert\mathbf{A}_{la}^{+}\rVert_2=1/\sigma_{\min}\leq
1/(\tau\sigma_{\max})$. Measured relative to the operator norm, the
pseudoinverse can amplify by at most $1/\tau$. That is the amplification the
truncation exists to limit, and $\tau$ sets it directly.\\
For the geometry of Section \ref{sec:radon} this keeps $11\,150$ of $16\,384$ directions. The numerical
null space $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$, which is the space the Nullspace Network operates on, has
dimension $16\,384-11\,150=5\,234$, roughly a third of image space. This is not a
small correction channel but a substantial subspace, and every statement of
Section \ref{sec:nsn} about what the Nullspace Network can and cannot repair
refers to this splitting of $\mathbb{R}^{16\,384}$.\\
Four implementation details are worth recording.\\
First, the pseudoinverse is never stored by itself. Writing $\mathbf{U}_k,\Sigma_k,
\mathbf{V}_k$ for the retained factors, the reconstruction is evaluated as the
two matrix products
$$\mathbf{A}_{la}^+\mathbf{y} \;=\; \mathbf{V}_k\,\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}\mathbf{y},$$
applied to a batch of measurement vectors. 
%TODO: better reason for the factors? otherwise it might be worth, to also store A and apply it directly for simplicity
We apply $\mathbf{A}_{la}^+$ in factored form because the factors are needed
anyway, so storing the pseudoinverse in addition would buy nothing. The two
factors earn their keep for different reasons, and it is worth separating them.
$\mathbf{V}_k$ alone carries both image-domain projections: the null-space
projector is $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$ and the error
decomposition of Section \ref{sec:nsn} is the same product, so neither of them
ever touches $\mathbf{U}_k$. $\mathbf{U}_k$ is needed for the other two
operations: the pseudoinverse itself, and the projection
%TODO: Isnt the range projection implemented as ID - nullspace projection?
$\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}=\mathbf{U}_k\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$
onto the retained range, which is what restricts the simulated noise of
Section \ref{sec:data} and the adversarial perturbation of Section
\ref{sec:adversarial} to sinograms that are measurements of some image. Both of
those act in sinogram space, which is why they cannot be expressed through
$\mathbf{V}_k$.\\
Second, the space the Nullspace Network acts in is the numerical null space of the truncated operator, the
$5\,234$-dimensional space counted above, and the space every decomposition
figure of Chapter \ref{sec:results} splits along. It too is applied without forming an
$n\times n$ matrix. The error decomposition of Section \ref{sec:nsn} is computed
the same way: the nullspace-complement component is
$\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}=\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}\mathbf{e}$ and the
nullspace component is
$\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}=\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$. The two are
orthogonal by construction, so
$\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}\rVert^2+
\lVert\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}\rVert^2$, which is the identity every decomposition
figure in Chapter \ref{sec:results} relies on.\\
Third, and decisively for this work, all of these operations are ordinary tensor
products, so PyTorch differentiates them. The gradient of an attack loss with
respect to the sinogram has to travel backwards through the initial
reconstruction into the measurements, and it is only because the pseudoinverse
is two matrix multiplications that this is possible at all.\\
Finally, precision. The matrix and its decomposition are built and cached
in double precision, but at training and attack time they are cast to single
precision and held densely, so that every application runs as a dense matrix
%TODO: Build everything in single precision and adapt following paragraph
product on the GPU. The sparse kernels are considerably slower for matrices of
this density --- $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ has on the order of one per cent of its
entries nonzero, which is far too dense for a sparse kernel to pay off on a GPU
--- and their automatic differentiation support is still experimental.\\
Building everything in single precision from the start would be simpler, and it
is deliberately not done. The decomposition is the one step where the precision
matters: the spectrum of $\mathbf{A}_{la}$ spans several orders of magnitude and
the cutoff sits at $\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$ of the largest singular value, so a
single-precision decomposition --- machine epsilon $\approx1.2\cdot10^{-7}$ ---
would place the truncation boundary, and with it the dimension of the null space
every result in this thesis is decomposed along, at the mercy of rounding.
Deciding the precision on load rather than at construction also lets the double
precision data generator and the single precision training and attack runs share
one cache, which they do: the cache key deliberately hashes the geometry and the
truncation threshold but not the dtype.
This matters during training and not only during the attacks: the Nullspace
Network applies $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ on every forward pass, so
it is a per-step cost of training rather than a one-off.

\subsection{Data generation}\label{sec:data}
The dataset consists of $5\,000$ phantoms, each containing a single random
ellipse, generated with ODL's \texttt{ellipsoid\_phantom} on the image grid
supplied by DIVAL's ellipse dataset. ODL rasterises the ellipse, DIVAL supplies the discretised image space it is
rasterised onto.
Intensity, semi-axes, centre and rotation
are drawn at random, with the semi-axes rejected until the ellipse has an area
of at least $0.1$ and the centre and rotation rejected until the ellipse lies
entirely inside the image. Restricting each image to one ellipse is a
deliberate simplification, it guarantees both
architectures reach a small clean reconstruction error.
This is needed to perform meaning full attacks, since we want to show, that the attack can manipulate an otherwise good reconstruction.
%TODO:Explain visual interpretability
It also keeps the null-space component of an error
visually interpretable, which a cluttered phantom would not.\\
For every sample the pipeline performs four steps.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draw the ground truth $\mathbf{x}^\dagger$.
    \item Simulate the noise-free measurement $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}_{la}\mathbf{x}^\dagger$.
    \item Add noise. A standard normal tensor is drawn on the sinogram and
    projected onto $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$, giving
    $\boldsymbol{\eta}$; it is then rescaled so that the perturbation has a
    prescribed \emph{relative} size,
    $$\mathbf{y}^\delta=\mathbf{y}+\sigma_\text{rel}
    \frac{\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}\rVert_2}\boldsymbol{\eta},
    \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
    \lVert\mathbf{y}^\delta-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2=\sigma_\text{rel}\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2$$
    holds exactly and per sample, independently of how bright the phantom is.
    \item Compute the initial reconstruction
    $\mathbf{x}_\text{init}=\mathbf{A}_{la}^+\mathbf{y}^\delta$ and store ground
    truth, sinogram and initial reconstruction to disk.
\end{enumerate}
%TODO: Do we need the 3 Different spaces and is there actually a difference between range A_la and range U_k, i though A_la always refers to the truncated operator so they should be the same, right?
%TODO: can we make a small test to investigate the difference between two implementations, where we use the truncated operator for inversion and the untruncated operator for data generationand in the other we just always use the truncated operator
The projection in step 3 is what the phrase ``restricted to the measured
angles'' has to mean here, and it is the reason two different operators appear in
the four steps above. Three nested subspaces of sinogram space are involved:
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M})$, every sinogram that is zero at the
unmeasured angles; $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$, those of them that
are the measurement of some image (the space of measurements); and the retained range
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{U}_k)$, the part of that which survives the
truncation at $\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$. The noise is projected onto the space of measurements,
using an essentially untruncated operator ($\tau=10^{-15}$), and the
reconstruction of step 4 inverts the retained range.\\
Drawing the noise in $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{M})$ instead would be wrong
for a reason of principle. A component of the measured rows
that no image could have produced is not measurement noise, it is a sinogram no
scanner can emit, and $\mathbf{A}_{la}^{+}$ discards it outright, so the nominal
$\sigma_\text{rel}$ would overstate the perturbation the reconstruction actually
receives. Drawing it in the retained range instead would be wrong in the
opposite direction: it would confine the noise to exactly the subspace the
reconstruction inverts, which flatters the method by construction. The space of measurements
is the honest one. It does not make every unit of the budget a unit the
reconstruction feels --- the components below the truncation threshold are
suppressed by $\mathbf{A}_{la}^{+}$ just as they would be in any regularised
inversion --- but every unit of it is a perturbation a scanner could have
produced, which is the property the noise model needs.\\
Note also that the noise is added after the measurement rather than to the photon
counts before it. Physically neither is faithful --- real CT noise is Poisson on
counts, before the log transform --- but the post-measurement Gaussian is the
standard idealisation of an inverse problem, and it is what makes the identity
above hold exactly and per sample.\\
Alongside the data the pipeline writes a summary file recording the geometry,
the angle vector, the limited-angle window, the noise level, the mean sinogram
norm, the mean noise norm, the operator norm and the truncation threshold.
Training and attacking both read their configuration from this file rather than
from the command line, which guarantees that the operator used to attack a model
is the operator that generated the data it was trained on.\\
Four noise levels are used, $\sigma_\text{rel}\in\{0.005,\,0.01,\,0.02,\,0.05\}$, each with its own dataset,
its own pair of trained models and its own attack run. The first $4\,000$
samples form the training set and the following $1\,000$ the test set; the split
is by index and therefore deterministic.

\subsection{Residual UNet}\label{sec:training}
To be able to describe the residual UNet, we first need to implement the UNet itself. We start the implementation of the UNet by defining a double convolution:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=DoubleConv]
class DoubleConv(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels, mid_channels=None):
        super().__init__()
        mid_channels = mid_channels or out_channels
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(in_channels, mid_channels, 3, padding=1, bias=False),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Conv2d(mid_channels, out_channels, 3, padding=1, bias=False),
            nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)
\end{lstlisting}
This applies two convolutions: the first takes the channel count to
$\texttt{mid\_channels}$, the second from there to $\texttt{out\_channels}$.
Left unspecified the two are equal, so the first convolution sets the channel
count and the second leaves it unchanged.
The expansive path below is the one place where it is set explicitly, and it is
the reason the argument exists at all. In the expansive path, the concatenation doubles the
channel count and the double convolution has to bring it down again in two
steps rather than one. Kernel size is $3$, padding is $1$ and there is no bias in these convolutions.
Each convolution is followed by a ReLU activation function. The parameter $\texttt{inplace}=\texttt{true}$ has the activation function modify the input tensor instead of generating a new output tensor. This decreases memory requirements.
We can then use this double convolution, to create a down and an upsampling
module.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=Down and Up]
class Down(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.MaxPool2d(2),
            DoubleConv(in_channels, out_channels),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)

class Up(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels):
        super().__init__()
        self.up = nn.Upsample(scale_factor=2, mode="bilinear",
                              align_corners=True)
        self.conv = DoubleConv(in_channels, out_channels, in_channels // 2)
    def forward(self, x1, x2):
        x1 = self.up(x1)
        diffY, diffX = x2.size(2) - x1.size(2), x2.size(3) - x1.size(3)
        x1 = F.pad(x1, [diffX // 2, diffX - diffX // 2,
                        diffY // 2, diffY - diffY // 2])
        return self.conv(torch.cat([x2, x1], dim=1))
\end{lstlisting}
The downsampling ($\texttt{Down}$) is just the $2\times2$ max pooling of
Section \ref{sec:resunet}, followed by a double convolution which is what raises
the channel count. The upsampling ($\texttt{Up}$) is bilinear upsampling with a
scale factor of $2$, so it doubles the resolution, and it is the counterpart of
that max pooling: pooling keeps one of every four values, bilinear upsampling
restores the resolution by interpolating between the values that survived.
Bilinear here means linear in each of the two image axes separately. A new pixel
falls between four known ones; interpolating linearly along the first axis
between each of the two pairs gives two intermediate values, and interpolating
linearly between those along the second axis gives the new pixel. The result is
a weighted average of the four neighbours whose weights are the products of the
one-dimensional distances. The
parameter $\texttt{align\_corners}=\texttt{True}$ fixes the convention for where
the known samples sit: the corner pixels of input and output are made to
coincide, so the corner values are reproduced exactly and the interpolation grid
is stretched to fit between them.\\
The upsampled tensor is then concatenated with the tensor of the corresponding
downsampling step --- this is the skip connection of the original U-Net
\parencite{U-NET}. The concatenation doubles the channel count, and the double
convolution it is passed through undoes that doubling: its intermediate channel
count is set to half its input, which is exactly the
$\texttt{mid\_channels}$ argument of $\texttt{DoubleConv}$. Should the two
tensors disagree in size by a pixel, which happens for odd resolutions, the
upsampled one is padded symmetrically before the concatenation; at our
resolutions, all powers of two, this never triggers.\\
A final $1\times1$ convolution ($\texttt{OutConv}$) reduces the $64$ feature
channels back to the single output channel. A $1\times1$ kernel has no spatial
extent, so it does not mix neighbouring pixels at all: at each pixel
independently it takes the $64$ channel values as a vector and returns one
weighted sum of them, with bias. It is a learned linear combination across
channels, applied identically at every pixel. This collapses a feature stack into one greyscale image.
The UNet definition is then the following:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=UNET]
class UNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.inc = DoubleConv(1, 64)
        self.down1 = Down(64, 128)
        self.down2 = Down(128, 256)
        self.down3 = Down(256, 512)
        self.down4 = Down(512, 512)
        self.up1 = Up(1024, 256)
        self.up2 = Up(512, 128)
        self.up3 = Up(256, 64)
        self.up4 = Up(128, 64)
        self.outc = OutConv(64, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        x1 = self.inc(x)
        x2 = self.down1(x1)
        x3 = self.down2(x2)
        x4 = self.down3(x3)
        x5 = self.down4(x4)
        x = self.up1(x5, x4)
        x = self.up2(x, x3)
        x = self.up3(x, x2)
        x = self.up4(x, x1)
        return self.outc(x)
\end{lstlisting}
Four downsampling steps take a $128\times128$ input
down to an $8\times8$ bottleneck carrying $512$ channels, and the expansive path
returns it to the original resolution. In total the network has $17\,252\,993$
trainable parameters. The count follows from the shapes alone, since every
parameter sits in a convolution kernel: a $3\times3$ convolution from $c_\text{in}$
to $c_\text{out}$ channels without bias holds $9\,c_\text{in}c_\text{out}$
weights, so a $\texttt{DoubleConv}(c_\text{in},c_\text{out},c_\text{mid})$ holds
$9(c_\text{in}c_\text{mid}+c_\text{mid}c_\text{out})$, and $\texttt{OutConv}$
adds $64\cdot1+1=65$ for its kernel and bias. Summing the ten blocks gives

This gives us an image to image neural network, that we can use in our residual and nullspace network architectures.
The output of the residual network is then calculated as $\mathbf{x} + \texttt{UNet}(\mathbf{x})$,

Let us now talk about how we train the network. The loss function we use is the mean squared error:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=mse\_loss]
def mse_loss(pred: torch.Tensor, target: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
  """training objective: MSE vs ground truth"""
  return torch.mean((pred - target) ** 2)
\end{lstlisting}
Where the tensor $\texttt{pred}$ is the network reconstruction and $\texttt{target}$ is the ground truth. We use an $\texttt{torch.optim.Adam}$ optimizer on the model parameters to train each epoch. In PyTorch the optimizer is created as

\begin{center}
\texttt{torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.0001)}.
\end{center}
Before each step we set the gradient to zero, recompute it with $\texttt{loss.backward()}$ and update the models parameters using the optimizer accordingly. We train for $50$ Epochs with a batch size of $32$, without a learning-rate
schedule and without data augmentation.\\
Both are standard devices and both are deliberately omitted. A learning-rate
schedule lowers the step size as training proceeds --- a large rate early to
cross the loss landscape quickly, a small one late to settle into a minimum
instead of bouncing around it --- and typically buys a somewhat lower final loss.
Data augmentation enlarges the training set with label-preserving
transformations of it, rotations or flips of the phantom here, and typically
buys a smaller generalisation gap. Neither is used because neither would serve
the question this work asks. Both architectures are trained by the identical
loop, so any accuracy either device bought would be bought for both, while the
comparison of the two is what the results rest on; and the epoch study of
Section \ref{sec:epoch} reads attackability against the epoch index, which a
changing learning rate would confound. Adding either is the obvious thing to try
if the clean reconstruction error ever became the bottleneck.\\
Each epoch is followed by an evaluation over the test set and whenever the validation loss improves, the weights are written
to disk. \\
Two further artifacts are produced during training, both of them for the epoch
study of Section \ref{sec:epoch}. Every $N$th epoch a snapshot of the weights is
written, and at the end of training a history file recording the training and
validation loss of each epoch together with the index of the best one.\\
The stride $N$ is a storage and runtime dial, not an accuracy one. One snapshot
of $17.3$ million single-precision parameters is $69$\,MB, so $N=1$ costs
$3.4$\,GB per model and noise level, $27$\,GB over the two architectures and
four noise levels; and because the epoch study attacks every snapshot it
writes, the same factor reappears in the runtime of that study. All experiments
in this work use $N=1$, which is to say the dial is left at its finest setting
and every epoch is both stored and attacked. Dropping it provides no benefit,
so for ease of reproducibility, it is kept.
The history file is what
lets the study plot attackability against the loss curves, so it is always
written regardless of $N$. Since the two architectures differ only in the
wrapper around the UNet, one training loop serves both.

\subsection{Nullspace Network}
The Nullspace Network shares the UNet of the previous section exactly, the two
architectures differ only in the application of the UNet correction.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The two wrappers]
class RESNET(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.unet(x)

class NSN(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.radon.proj_null_image(self.unet(x))
\end{lstlisting}
Here $\texttt{proj\_null\_image}$ is the operator
$\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$ of
Section \ref{sec:pinv}, so the second wrapper is exactly the reconstruction map
$\mathbf{x}_\text{out}=\mathbf{x}_\text{init}+
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$ of Section
\ref{sec:nsn}.\\
%TODO: It seems this is kind of obvious? should we even state that?
The projection sits \emph{inside} the computational graph and not after it. One could obtain a data
consistent reconstruction from the unconstrained residual network too, simply by
projecting its output once training had finished. Section \ref{sec:nsn} does not
distinguish the two, because at inference they produce the same map. Training
does distinguish them: in the post-hoc variant the network is optimised to
produce a correction it is then not allowed to make, whereas here it never sees a
reward for the part of its output that would be discarded.\\
That is a consequence of the chain rule. Write $\mathbf{r}=N_\theta(\mathbf{x}_\text{init})$
for the raw network output, so that the reconstruction is
$\mathbf{x}_\text{out}=\mathbf{x}_\text{init}+\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{r}$.
Differentiating the loss through that last linear step transposes the operator,
$$\nabla_{\!\mathbf{r}}\mathcal{L}
=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}^{*}\,\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}_\text{out}}\mathcal{L}
=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}_\text{out}}\mathcal{L},$$
the second equality because an orthogonal projector is self-adjoint,
$\mathbf{P}^{*}=\mathbf{P}$. The gradient that arrives at the UNet output is
therefore itself a null-space vector: whatever the loss would like to change
about the component of $\mathbf{r}$ in $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})^{\perp}$, no
gradient signal to that effect ever reaches the weights. The network is not

constrained after the fact; it is trained only on the subspace it is permitted to
act in.\\
That the implementation actually realises the properties claimed in Section

\ref{sec:nsn} is checked numerically against the real operator, and the precise
form of those checks is worth stating, because truncation weakens one of them.
The test suite verifies that $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ is
idempotent, that the error decomposition reproduces the error exactly and splits
it orthogonally, and that the correction of a Nullspace Network is invisible to
the measurements. The data-consistency check is not
$\mathbf{A}_{la}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$, which under truncation
is false: $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^{*}$
retains the discarded small-singular-value directions, and $\mathbf{A}_{la}$
maps those to something small but nonzero. What does vanish exactly, and what the
test asserts, is the \emph{measured} trace,
$$\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}\,\mathbf{A}_{la}\,
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0,$$
which is the identity the reconstruction pipeline actually relies on, since that
projection is what every measurement in this work passes through. One further
point: the check is made with untrained weights and holds for any weights at
all, because the property is a property of the architecture and not of what the
network has learned.

\subsection{Adversarial attacks}\label{sec:adversarial}
In the attack implementation we used, we differentiate the whole chain from the sinogram to
the reconstruction,
$$\mathbf{y}\;\longmapsto\;\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}\mathbf{y}
\;\longmapsto\;\mathbf{x}_\text{init}=\mathbf{A}_{la}^+\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}\mathbf{y}
\;\longmapsto\;\mathcal{F}_\theta(\mathbf{y}),$$
where $\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}=\mathbf{U}_k\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$
is the orthogonal projector onto the retained range of the limited-angle
operator. It is emphatically \emph{not} the mask $\mathbf{M}$ of the convention
above.
Separating the initial reconstruction from the network in the
code means that the same attack implementation drives both architectures with
no branching inside the attack itself.\\
%TODO: Paragraph until budget still to investigate
The first arrow also applies to the clean sinogram before the attack begins:
what is attacked is $\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}\mathbf{y}^\delta$
rather than the $\mathbf{y}^\delta$ that was written to disk. This changes
nothing about the network input, because
$\mathbf{A}_{la}^+=\mathbf{V}_k\Sigma_k^{-1}\mathbf{U}_k^{*}$ annihilates exactly
the component the projection removes, so
$\mathbf{A}_{la}^+\mathbf{y}^\delta=\mathbf{A}_{la}^+
\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}\mathbf{y}^\delta$ identically;
it matters only in that the reported perturbation is measured against the
projected sinogram.\\\\
\textbf{Budget.} The budget is relative and per sample:
$\varepsilon_i=\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{y}_i\rVert_2$, so a bright and
a faint sinogram are attacked at the same relative strength. Unless stated
otherwise $\varepsilon$ is set to $\sigma_\text{rel}$, the relative noise level
of Section \ref{sec:data} for the dataset the model was trained on, so that the
attacker is granted exactly as much energy as the
measurement noise the model has already learned to tolerate. A perturbation that
does real damage within that budget is a perturbation indistinguishable in size
from noise the scanner produces anyway.\\\\
\textbf{Step size and restarts.} The step size is
$$\alpha = \frac{2.5\,\varepsilon\,\bar{y}}{T},\qquad T=50,$$
%TODO: Check reference for customary rule
with $\bar y$ the mean sinogram norm of the dataset, that is, the customary
$2.5\,\varepsilon/T$ rule for PGD \parencite{PGD} expressed in absolute units:
the factor $\bar y$ converts the relative budget into the units the sinogram is
measured in, and the factor $2.5$ leaves the iteration enough total travel to
cross the ball and still refine inside it. Each attack is
run from $2$ independent random starting points and the run achieving the higher
objective is kept.\\
%TODO: Implement per alpha stepsize and introduce it properly
One asymmetry is worth recording, because it is a property of the
implementation and not of the method. The budget is per sample,
$\varepsilon_i=\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{y}_i\rVert_2$, while the step size uses
the dataset mean $\bar y$ and is therefore the same absolute length for every
sample in a batch. Relative to its own budget a faint sinogram is thus stepped
slightly more coarsely than a bright one. Since the projection onto
$B_{\varepsilon_i}$ is applied after every step the iterates stay feasible
regardless, and with $50$ iterations and $2$ restarts the effect on the attained
objective is small, but a per-sample $\alpha_i$ would be the cleaner choice.\\\\
\textbf{Feasible set.} Writing $\mathbf{P}$ for
$\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}$, the projection $\Pi_S$ of
Section \ref{sec:attacks} is implemented as
$$\Pi_S(\boldsymbol{\delta})=\mathbf{P}\,
\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\delta}\right),
\qquad
\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}\cdot
\min\!\left(1,\frac{\varepsilon}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_2}\right),$$
with the ball projection applied per sample.\\
The outer $\mathbf{P}$ is, mathematically, redundant. $\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}$
multiplies each sample by a scalar, and scaling a vector cannot take it out of a
subspace, so $\Pi_{B_\varepsilon}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\delta})$ already lies in
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$ and the second $\mathbf{P}$ changes
nothing. It is applied anyway for a numerical reason: the iterate is projected
and rescaled once per step for $50$ steps in single precision, and without the
re-projection the small component that rounding introduces outside the subspace
accumulates over those steps. The cost is one additional pair of matrix products
per step against an attack that already pays several, and it keeps the
invariant the test suite asserts --- that the returned $\boldsymbol{\delta}$
satisfies $\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}$ --- true to
single-precision tolerance rather than approximately.\\
%TODO: Check truncation and untruncation application, i think one unified use would be of great advantage
The feasible set of our attacks is therefore
$$S:=\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})\cap B_\varepsilon.$$
The simulated measurement noise of Section \ref{sec:data} is drawn from the same
kind of set but not from the identical one, and the difference is not an
oversight. Both are projections onto a retained range; they differ in which
truncation threshold defines it. The noise is projected at $\tau=10^{-15}$ and so
occupies $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$ in full, all $16\,384$
directions, whereas the attack is projected at the operating threshold
$\tau=4\cdot10^{-3}$ and so occupies only the $11\,150$ retained ones. The reason
is that the two projections answer different questions. The noise model asks what
a scanner could physically emit, which is all of
$\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})$. The threat model asks what an attacker
can spend a budget on to change the reconstruction, and energy placed in the
discarded directions is suppressed by $\mathbf{A}_{la}^{+}$, so allowing it would
only let the attacker waste the budget. The attacker is therefore confined to a
strict subspace of the space the noise lives in, which is if anything a handicap
and does not flatter the robustness results.\\
With these pieces the iteration of Section \ref{sec:attacks} is ten lines of
code. In the listing below, $\texttt{project\_delta}$ is the projection
$\Pi_S$ given above, and $\texttt{adapter.projector}$ is $\mathbf{P}$.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The PGD loop]
for _ in range(SUITE_RESTARTS):
    delta = adapter.projector(proj_l2_ball(torch.randn_like(y_clean), eps))
    delta = project_delta(delta, eps, adapter.projector)

    for _ in range(SUITE_STEPS):
        y_adv = (y_clean + delta).detach().requires_grad_(True)
        pred, _, _ = adapter.forward(y_adv, project=False)
        grad = torch.autograd.grad(loss_of(pred), y_adv)[0]
        with torch.no_grad():
            g = grad / l2_norm_batch(grad).clamp_min(1e-12).view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
            delta = project_delta(delta + alpha * g, eps, adapter.projector)
\end{lstlisting}
Three details of the listing correspond to the derivation. The gradient is
taken with respect to the perturbed sinogram $\texttt{y\_adv}$ rather than the
perturbation, which is the same gradient because the two differ by the constant
$\mathbf{y}$, and it is the form autograd needs since

$\texttt{y\_adv}$ is the leaf of the graph. The step is \emph{added}, so that the
loss is maximised. And the gradient is normalised in the $\ell_2$ norm before the step,
exactly as derived in Section \ref{sec:attacks}. The model is held in evaluation mode
throughout and gradients are requested only with respect to the input, so no
weight is ever modified by an attack.\\
Five attacks are run over every model, differing only in the loss that
$\texttt{loss\_of}$ evaluates: the untargeted attack on the total error, the two
subspace attacks of the next section, and the two targeted attacks of the one
after that.
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks on Subspaces}
The two subspace losses of Section \ref{sec:subspace} are direct transcriptions of their
definitions.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The subspace objectives]
if objective == "null":
    err = pred - x_gt
    return reduce_loss(radon.proj_null_image(err) ** 2)

if objective == "range":
    err = pred - x_gt
    return reduce_loss((err - radon.proj_null_image(err)) ** 2)
\end{lstlisting}
Up to the constant factor by which a mean differs from a squared norm, the first is $\mathcal{L}_\mathcal{N}$ and the second
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$. The constant factor of course does not affect the direction of the gradient.
Both use the same projector $\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}$ as the
Nullspace Network itself, and for an attack on that network it therefore occurs
twice in the graph --- once inside the model, where it constrains the correction,
and once inside the loss, where it selects the component being maximised --- and
is differentiated both times. For an attack on the residual network it occurs
only in the loss.\\
Applying it once costs two matrix products against $\mathbf{V}_k$, namely
$\mathbf{V}_k^{*}\mathbf{v}$ followed by $\mathbf{V}_k(\cdot)$, that is
$2kn\approx3.7\cdot10^{8}$ multiply-accumulates per sample, where
$k=11\,150$ is the number of retained singular directions and $n=16\,384$ the
number of pixels. Against the Nullspace Network the attack pays that four times
per step --- twice in the forward pass and twice again in the backward pass ---
for $50$ iterations and $2$ restarts on every sample. It remains affordable
because these are dense single-precision products on the GPU, which is what the
precision and layout choices of Section \ref{sec:pinv} were made for: the same
%TODO: Reference for this claim
products in double precision would run at a fraction of the single-precision
throughput of this GPU class, and against the sparse operator they would in
addition pay the irregular memory access of a CSR kernel on a matrix that is far
too dense to benefit from one. 
\subsubsection{Targeted Adversarial Attacks using Examples}
The two targeted losses of Section \ref{sec:targeted} are negated squared distances, so that
maximising them moves the reconstruction towards the target.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=The targeted objectives]
if objective == "zero":
    return -reduce_loss(pred ** 2)

if objective == "target":
    return -reduce_loss((pred - target.detach()) ** 2)
\end{lstlisting}
The first realises $\mathbf{t}=0$ and needs no reference image at all. The
second does, and it is supplied once per batch: the target of each sample is the
ground truth of a different sample of the same batch, so no extra data is needed
and the targets are as plausible as the phantoms themselves.\\
The targets are assigned by a derangement of the batch indices --- a permutation
that leaves no index in place. It is drawn by rejection: a random permutation is
sampled and redrawn if it fixes any index, with a cyclic shift used as a
fallback in the rare case that twenty draws all fail. The point is that no sample
may be steered towards its own ground truth, which would turn the targeted
attack into no attack at all for that sample and would quietly weaken the
reported numbers.\\
The target enters the loss as $\texttt{target.detach()}$. Detaching a tensor
returns a view of it that is cut out of the computational graph, so no gradient
can flow back through it. Here that is defensive rather than necessary: the
target is ground truth read from disk and carries no graph to begin with. It
states that the target is a fixed reference and not a quantity the attack may
optimise, and it keeps that true if the target were ever computed from something
differentiable.
\subsubsection{Epoch study}\label{sec:epoch}
The epoch study investigates, whether there is a specific time during training, when a model becomes attackable.
This is helpful to decide, whether attackability coincides with the onset of
overfitting. It consumes the epoch
snapshots written by the training loop of Section \ref{sec:training}. For each architecture,
every snapshot is loaded in turn, attacked with the untargeted
squared-error objective at a fixed budget of
$\varepsilon_\text{rel}=0.01$, and evaluated. The study writes its own table,
one row per snapshot, carrying the adversarial error of that snapshot next to
the training and validation loss which it reads back from the history file of
Section \ref{sec:training}. The two quantities therefore end up on one axis
without the training loop and the attack having to know anything about each
other. Because the study repeats a full attack once per snapshot, it runs on $32$
test samples rather than the $128$ used for the other attacks.\\
The inputs are cached once and every snapshot is attacked on that same cache.
This matters for reading the resulting curve: the phantoms, their sinograms and
their initial reconstructions are byte-identical from epoch to epoch, so the
only quantity that varies along the curve is the weights, and any trend in it is
a trend in the model rather than in the sample of test data it was measured on.

\subsection{Metrics and error decomposition}
Every attacked sample is scored with the same set of seven metrics, each
computed between a reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and the ground truth
$\mathbf{x}^\dagger$. No single one of them is sufficient, which is why all seven
are kept: they disagree about what counts as damage, and an attack that looks
mild under one can be severe under another.
%TODO: Check error definitions
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Relative $\ell_2$ error},
$\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2/
\lVert\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2$. The headline number of this work, because it
is the quantity the error decomposition below is additive in. Normalising by
$\lVert\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rVert_2$ makes it comparable across phantoms of
different brightness; the denominator is floored, since a phantom whose ellipse
falls below the sampling grid is all zero and would otherwise blow the ratio up.
\item \textbf{RMSE}, $\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}((\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger)^2)}$,
the same error unnormalised, in image units. Kept because the relative error
hides how bright an artefact actually is.
\item \textbf{NRMSE}, the RMSE divided by the data range
$\max\mathbf{x}^\dagger-\min\mathbf{x}^\dagger$. A second normalisation, by
contrast rather than by energy.
\item \textbf{PSNR}, $20\log_{10}(\text{range})-10\log_{10}(\text{MSE})$ in
decibels. The same information as the RMSE on a logarithmic scale, reported
because it is the conventional currency of the reconstruction literature.
\item \textbf{SSIM}, structural similarity. The only metric here that is not a
function of the pixelwise difference alone: it compares local means, variances
and covariances, so it responds to structure being moved or invented rather than
merely to intensity being wrong. That is exactly the failure mode a targeted
attack aims for, and the one a pure $\ell_2$ figure understates.
\item \textbf{MAE}, $\operatorname{mean}\lvert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rvert$.
Less sensitive to a few large deviations than the squared metrics, so a gap
between MAE and RMSE indicates that the error is concentrated rather than spread.
\item \textbf{Maximum absolute error},
$\max\lvert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^\dagger\rvert$. The opposite extreme: it
sees only the single worst pixel. A small mean error with a large maximum is a
localised artefact, which is the clinically relevant kind.
\end{itemize}
All seven are recorded for the clean and for the adversarial reconstruction, and
separately for the initial reconstruction, so that the damage the attack does to
the network input can be told apart from the damage it does to the output. 
Aggregation reports the mean with a $95\%$ confidence interval of half-width
$1.96\,s/\sqrt{N}$, where $s$ is the sample standard deviation of the metric over
the $N$ attacked samples and $1.96$ the normal quantile, together with the median
and the quartiles. The interval is reported because the headline comparison is a
comparison of two means over $N=128$ samples, and a difference smaller than the
two intervals is not a difference at all; it quantifies the sampling error of
this particular test set, not the variability of the attack. The median and
quartiles are reported alongside because the distribution over samples is
skewed --- a handful of samples on which an attack succeeds unusually well would
otherwise dominate the mean --- so a gap between mean and median is itself
informative.\\
In addition, every error field is decomposed as in Section \ref{sec:pinv} into
$\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$, and the norms of
both components as well as their fractions of $\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert$ are
recorded. Since only the squared $\ell_2$ norm is additive over this
decomposition, the remaining metrics cannot be split the same way.
Instead the component-only images $\mathbf{x}^\dagger+\mathbf{e}_{\mathcal{N}^\perp}$ and
$\mathbf{x}^\dagger+\mathbf{e}_\mathcal{N}$ are scored with the same metrics,
which shows how much of the loss in, say, SSIM each subspace is responsible for
on its own. The construction deserves a word of caution: these are not
reconstructions any method produced, they are the ground truth with one component
of an error added back, and the two numbers do not add up to the number for the
full reconstruction, because SSIM and PSNR are not additive over the
decomposition --- that is precisely why the construction is needed. They are to
be read as an attribution of the damage, not as a decomposition of the score.\\
Data consistency is measured as the relative residual of the reconstruction
against the measurement it should explain,
$$\frac{\lVert\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{A}_{la})}
\mathbf{A}_{la}\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2}
{\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2}.$$
%TODO: Also check with truncation, i feel both implementation add unnecessary complexity through out the work
Two choices in that formula are deliberate. The operator is the limited-angle
$\mathbf{A}_{la}$, not the full one: the unmeasured angles carry no data to be
consistent with, so including them would score the reconstruction against
numbers no scanner produced. And the range projection is applied to
$\mathbf{A}_{la}\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ rather than omitted, which is not redundant
under truncation: $\mathbf{A}_{la}\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ may have a component in the
discarded small-singular-value directions, the sinograms $\mathbf{y}$ it is
compared against do not, and leaving that component in would add to the residual
a term that says nothing about data consistency. Both sides of the difference are
thus in the same subspace, the one the whole pipeline works in.\\
Two versions of this number are recorded and they mean quite different things.
Evaluated against the \emph{attacked} sinogram, the Nullspace Network drives it
to about $10^{-8}$ whatever the attack does --- that is the guarantee of Section
\ref{sec:nsn} restated, a tautology rather than a robustness result. Evaluated
against the \emph{clean} sinogram it reports how far the
attack has pushed the reconstruction away from the measurements actually taken and it is this version that carries information.\\
%TODO: Check following section
Finally, an attack-independent robustness measure is computed. Every number
above is the outcome of one particular attack, so none of them can distinguish a
robust model from one the attack merely failed to find a direction for. Write
$f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}$ for the image-to-image map the wrapper implements,
so $f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+N_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ for the residual network and
$f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}N_\theta(\mathbf{x})$
for the Nullspace Network, and let $g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}$ be
the learned correction alone. Linearising $g$ around the clean initial
reconstruction $\mathbf{x}_0$ and restricting both its input and its output to
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$ with the same projector gives
$$\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}\,
\mathbf{J}_g(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})},$$
whose largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{T})$ is the local gain of the
learned channel: the factor by which a worst-case null-space perturbation of the
input can be amplified into null-space error at the output. A value below one
means the correction contracts in that subspace, above one that it amplifies.\\
It is estimated by power iteration, and the Jacobian is never assembled. One
power-iteration step needs $\mathbf{T}\mathbf{d}$ and
$\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{u}$, which are exactly a Jacobian-vector and a
vector-Jacobian product of $g$ with a projection on each side, and PyTorch
supplies both directly:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption=One power iteration of the null-restricted gain]
def G(x):                      # learned correction, output restricted to N(A)
    return proj(model(x) - x)

d = proj(torch.randn_like(x0))
d = d / torch.linalg.norm(d.reshape(-1))
for _ in range(LIPSCHITZ_ITERS):
    _, u = torch.autograd.functional.jvp(G, x0, d, strict=False)
    _, w = torch.autograd.functional.vjp(G, x0, proj(u), strict=False)
    w = proj(w)
    d = w / torch.linalg.norm(w.reshape(-1))
_, u = torch.autograd.functional.jvp(G, x0, d, strict=False)
sigma = torch.linalg.norm(proj(u).reshape(-1))
\end{lstlisting}
Two features distinguish this from a plain Lipschitz estimate of the network.
It is \emph{local}: it is the gain of the linearisation at one point
$\mathbf{x}_0$, not a global bound over all inputs, so it describes the
neighbourhood the reconstruction actually lives in rather than a worst case over
image space --- which is what makes it comparable to an attack that also starts
there. And it is \emph{restricted}: input and output are both confined to
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})$. That restriction is what makes the number comparable
between the two architectures at all. Unrestricted, the Nullspace Network's
correction is identically zero on $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})^{\perp}$ while the
residual network's is not, so an unrestricted estimate would mostly measure the
architectural constraint rather than what either network has learned. Measured on
the subspace both are free to act in, the comparison is between the learned maps.
The estimate is averaged over several clean initial reconstructions, and the
maximum over them is reported alongside the mean.

\subsection{Experimental pipeline}
Computation and rendering are kept strictly apart. An attack run writes only
numeric artifacts: the per-sample metrics as a table, the adversarial sinograms
and perturbations, the example images with their per-sample metrics, the
cross-model transfer stacks and the aggregated summary. Every figure in Chapter
4 is produced afterwards from those files alone, without a model, a checkpoint or
a Radon operator being loaded. A figure can therefore be redrawn at any time
without repeating the attack that produced it, which matters because a full
attack run takes several GPU-hours.\\
The four noise levels are processed by a Slurm pipeline as four job arrays over
three dependency levels: data generation and training first; then the attack
suite and the epoch study, which both depend only on that first level and
therefore run concurrently; then the rendering, which waits for both. Because
the four noise levels are entirely independent of one another, each level of the
pipeline is an array over them rather than a loop, so the critical path is one
noise level deep rather than four.\\
Reproducibility rests on three things. The random seeds are fixed, both for data
generation and for the attacks. All models within a run are attacked on a cached
set of inputs, so that they see identical data and any difference between them is
a difference of architecture. And the operator identities the argument of this
thesis depends on --- the idempotence of the null-space projector,
$\mathbf{A}_{la}\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}=0$, the agreement of the
truncated factors with the sparse system matrix, the orthogonality of the
error decomposition and the enforcement of the attack budget --- are covered by a
test suite that exercises them against the real operator rather than a mock.
